## Core Themes

### Narrative Tone
- **Slice of life with depth**: Balances everyday moments with serious, impactful events
- **Real stakes**: Characters can and will die. Betrayals happen. There will also be great times for the characters.
- **Found family**: The player builds bonds that may become more important than returning home

### Tonal Reference
- **The Wandering Inn (Pirateaba)**: The closest published touchstone for the tone we want. Cozy daily texture (food, walking, work, conversation) coexisting with sudden real violence and real death. Earnest, not ironic. Deep character interiority. RPG-style mechanics treated as natural facts of the world. Long, patient pacing where a quiet chapter has equal weight to a battle chapter. Walking and travel as meaningful, not just connective tissue — which maps directly onto StepQuest's step-based core loop.

### What Drives Human Conflict
- The corruption does NOT affect human minds
- Fear, hunger, and desperation drive people to conflict
- Human nature under pressure - not magical mind control

### The Player's Motivation
- Primary: Wants to return to their own world, their home
- Secondary: As they bond with characters, that desire may fade or decrease
- Tertiary: Gets invested in the world's problems and people

## Magic System

### The Nature of Magic

**Origin**: Magic is a natural energy produced by the planet itself. It regenerates over time - even after being completely drained, the planet will slowly produce more over centuries and millennia.

**Ambient Magic**: Magic exists throughout the environment - atmosphere, ground, water. It permeates everything.

### How Magic Interacts with Life

**Passive Absorption**: All living beings naturally absorb ambient magic without conscious effort - humans, animals, plants, all life forms.

**Enhancement, Not Sustenance**: Magic does NOT sustain life. Creatures can survive without magic entirely. However, magic ENHANCES quality of life:
- Increased vitality and energy
- Sharper mental clarity
- Better physical health
- Improved crop yields
- General sense of well-being

**Clarification - Highly Magical Life**: The rule above describes ordinary surface life. Some highly magic-dependent subterranean creatures are a special case: lack of magic does not kill them outright, but can force them into deep slumber or biological suspension until ambient magic rises again.

**Symbiosis**: The relationship between the planet and its inhabitants is symbiotic. The planet produces magic; life benefits from it.

### Magic Users

- Magic exists and is accepted in society
- **In principle, almost anyone could learn magic. In practice, active magic use is rare** — progress is gated by *magical sensitivity*: the ability to perceive and grip ambient magic in the first place. Most people's sensitivity is too dull for practice to ever get anywhere
- **Sensitivity is developmental**: it is calibrated by the magical environment a body grows up in. Childhood sets the baseline; adult exposure does not recalibrate it. Natives raised alongside ambient magic build ordinary tolerance as they develop and end up with dulled senses for it
- **The remaining mystery**: why some natives are born or end up unusually sensitive despite an ordinary upbringing is not understood. This — not the existence of magic ability itself — is the open question scholars circle
- Magic is more common now than centuries ago, as ambient levels have been rising
- **How mages are formed**: you can either sense ambient magic or you can't — and if you can't, sensing itself can be trained (for most people, whose baseline sensitivity is too dull, training never gets anywhere). Once a person can sense magic, ability is built by learning to affect what they sense: molding it to their will. Practitioners struggle to describe the act; the nearest word is a kind of *pushing*

### Otherworlders and Magic

**Natural Affinity**: People from non-magical worlds have a natural affinity for magic. Having never been exposed to it, they never built up tolerance. This makes perceiving and learning to manipulate magic easier for them. This is distinct from natives born during the magic-poor centuries: those generations had no meaningful ambient magic to learn from or practice with, and later native generations grew up alongside slowly rising magic levels, building ordinary tolerance as they developed. Otherworlders arrive with bodies and senses formed outside the planet's magic cycle, then encounter magic all at once. However, this affinity only affects active magic use — otherworlders absorb ambient magic passively at the same rate as anyone else. And because sensitivity is developmental — calibrated in childhood, never recalibrated by adult exposure (see Magic Users) — an otherworlder's zero-magic baseline is locked in for life: the affinity does not fade, no matter how long they live in this world or how much ambient magic they passively absorb.

**Not an Intended Gift**: This affinity is a natural consequence of their origin, not something the ancients deliberately gave them. While the ancients noted otherworlders' natural talent during the brief summoning period before the seal, this affinity became irrelevant once the seal took effect - there was no magic left to sense or use. Only now, with magic returning, would this trait actually matter.

**Not Heritable**: This affinity applies only to first-generation otherworlders - those actually summoned from non-magical worlds. Their children, born in a world with magic, develop normal tolerance like anyone else, even if ambient levels are low at the time of birth. Ancient records note that summoned people showed natural talent with magic, but their descendants displayed no such gift.

### Corrupted Magic

**Effects on Living Beings** (who passively absorb it):
- Makes humans sick (physical illness)
- Makes animals sick, which causes them to become violent and aggressive (pain response, not mind control)
- Causes crops to fail

**Visibility**: Corrupted magic affects ambient mana, not the physical landscape. The terrain itself is not visibly damaged or discolored. The effects are biological - sickness in living beings, crop failure - not environmental destruction. There is no visible atmospheric pollution or magical fog.

**The Dead-Zone Exception**: While corruption itself is invisible, sustained exposure at very high concentration kills life outright — not just makes it sick. A region soaked in dense corruption for long enough loses its plants, its animals, and eventually any trace of living growth. The result *looks* dead: bare soil, dried-out tree skeletons, no birdsong, no insects, no game. The land is not magically tainted in itself, and the soil can support life again once corruption levels drop — but during the exposure, and for a long time after, it appears scorched. The historical extreme is the wide zone around the Original's lair in ancient times: high-concentration corruption was sustained there for years, and by the time the seal was cast, that whole area was visibly lifeless (see Spera → pre-Spera name "the Cinderlands").

**Important**: Corrupted magic does NOT affect minds directly - not humans, not animals. It does not control thoughts or alter personalities. Animals become aggressive because they are in pain and discomfort from the physical sickness. Psychological effects in humans (paranoia, violence, cruelty) come from human reactions to the physical crisis - sickness, hunger, fear.

**How Harm Scales — the Saturation Rule**: The damage corruption does to a living body scales with the corrupted *fraction* of the magic that body absorbs, relative to its overall magical saturation. A weakly-magical surface creature, drawing on thin surface-level ambient magic, takes a proportionally large hit from even trace corruption — ordinary animals are therefore among the first visible signs of corruption in a region. A highly magical creature saturated in dense pure magic barely registers the same trace amounts. The rule is consistent with mage vulnerability: mages actively absorb large volumes of ambient magic, corrupted fraction included (see Mage Sensitivity to Corruption).

### Corrupted Magic Accumulation

**Persistence**: Corrupted magic accumulates indefinitely in the atmosphere. The planet only produces pure magic — it cannot cleanse or neutralize corruption. Pure and corrupted magic mix together in the ambient environment.

**Dilution**: Slow dilution over centuries is possible as the planet continues producing pure magic, but this is uncertain and not understood by people in the current era.

**Current Levels**: The corruption currently in the atmosphere is a tiny fraction of what existed during ancient times. The majority of crystallized corrupted magic is still unmined, sitting untouched in deposits across Spera.

### Mage Sensitivity to Corruption

**Detection**: Mages can detect corrupted magic, but only when present in sufficient concentration. At low levels, it is imperceptible.

**Vulnerability**: Mages are more vulnerable to corruption than ordinary people. When practicing magic, mages must actively absorb surrounding ambient magic to use their abilities and replenish their reserves. This is far more magic-intensive than the passive absorption all beings experience. As a result, mages accumulate corrupted magic in their bodies faster than non-mages.

**Local Mages**: Mages who have lived their entire lives in Spera do not notice the gradual change in quality of ambient magic — they grew up with it slowly worsening and have no baseline for comparison.

**Foreign Mages**: Mages visiting from other nations can sense that something is "wrong" with the ambient magic — a faint trace of something off. However, they will not suffer health consequences without prolonged exposure.

**Timeline of Effects**:
- **Years 1-20**: Low corruption levels, slow accumulation. Toward the end of this period, the most active mages began experiencing slight effects (headaches, tiredness) — easily attributed to other causes. Most mages and the general population noticed nothing.
- **Years 20-35 (present)**: Corruption concentration significantly higher. Most active mages feel real hindrance in their practice. Some mages who practiced intensively throughout the past 35 years now have mid-to-severe health issues (mostly mid, severe cases are rare). Regular mages are only beginning to experience what the most active mages felt 10-15 years ago. Some individuals in the general population are starting to show effects.

**Acceleration**: Corruption accumulation is not linear — it is accelerating. Years 1-20 saw much lower corruption than years 20-35. Now that ambient magic is more concentrated with corruption, effects will manifest faster. The rate at which people become affected is increasing.

### Corruption Magic (Practiced)

- The organism was not present long enough for anyone to learn how to use corrupted magic in any significant way
- People knew it was possible through crude experiments, but that was the extent of it
- Even these crude experiments were enough to cause physical harm to the mages who attempted them
- This never developed into a real discipline or practice

## The Alien Organism

### Origin

- Arrived on the planet via meteorite during the height of the ancient civilization
- The meteorite impact created a deep crater; the Original was at the bottom
- Over the millennia *after the seal*, the crater filled in naturally with sediment and rock (the organism was active for only ~2 years before the Death of Magic; the filling happened during the long ages of its hibernation)
- An alien lifeform - not of this world
- Evolved to survive space travel: vacuum, extreme temperatures, absence of magic

### Nature

- Semi-conscious, similar to a fungus or plant
- Not intelligent - does not think, plan, or scheme
- Simply exists, feeds, grows, and reproduces
- The Original is physically indestructible by any means the ancients possessed

### Feeding and Corruption

- **Awakening vs. feeding are different processes**: hibernation ends with exposure to ambient magic of any kind — pure or corrupted, it does not matter, because rousing from hibernation is biological reactivation, not metabolism. Once awake, however, it can only metabolize **pure magic**. Corrupted magic is its own waste product and useless to it as food
- When it consumes pure magic, it excretes corrupted magic as a byproduct
- This is its biological process - not intentional malice
- As it feeds, it saturates surroundings with corrupted magic

### Reproduction

- Reproduces via spores
- Spores grow into new organisms, which feed and release more spores
- Exponential growth pattern

### The Spores vs The Original

**The Spores**:
- NOT indestructible
- Could be killed, but required significant effort and specialized knowledge
- Not everyone could do it
- The problem was volume: too many, spreading too fast, everywhere
- Realistically impossible to eradicate them all
- Spores could be physically touched - they corrupted the magic they fed on, not physical matter directly
- However, proximity to spores caused nausea and illness due to the ambient corrupted magic they excreted

**The Original**:
- Physically indestructible
- Could survive without magic (evolved for space travel)
- When magic vanished, it entered hibernation rather than dying
- The only survivor of its kind on this planet
- Currently dormant

### Current Location

- The Original is entombed at the center of the largest violet crystal formation on the planet
- When the seal activated, the massive concentration of corrupted magic around it crystallized instantly
- It is trapped in crystallized magic - a prison created inadvertently by the seal
- This location is in the neighboring nation that began mining violet crystals
- The formation is currently underground, hidden beneath the filled-in crater
- It has NOT been discovered yet - Spera has been mining other, smaller deposits
- **Why It Remained Undiscovered**: After the Death of Magic, resettlement avoided the area around the lair — the survivors' warnings still carried weight at first, and life rebuilt itself elsewhere. As centuries passed, the original reason was forgotten, but the pattern persisted — the area was simply ignored because it held no interest. Settlement had taken root elsewhere, and this location was just a place filled with nature, not interesting enough to invest in or build upon.
- The largest deposit will be discovered during the story, when mining operations following nearby satellite veins and smaller surrounding deposits accidentally breach into the formation via tunnel

### Awakening Conditions

- Currently in deep hibernation
- The Original requires access to magic to awaken — any kind, pure or corrupted (see Feeding and Corruption)
- **The prison conducts like-to-like**: the prison is crystallized *corrupted* magic, and a medium passes its own kind. It insulates strongly (though imperfectly) against pure ambient magic, but it *conducts corrupted ambient magic inward*. This is why the planet's slow natural recovery — millennia of pure magic rising to ~50% of ancient levels — never meaningfully reached the Original, while Spera's 35 years of corrupted atmospheric leakage genuinely does
- **Two factors drive the awakening:**
  - **Physical approach toward the prison**: Spera's mining operations are removing nearby violet formations, following satellite veins, and digging closer to the prison without realizing what they are approaching. This gradually reduces the surrounding buffer. A direct breach would bypass the prison's selectivity entirely, exposing the Original to *all* ambient magic — pure and corrupted — at once
  - **Atmospheric corruption concentration**: even with the prison intact, rising *corrupted* ambient levels increase the rate at which magic is conducted through the prison's layers. The higher the atmosphere's corrupted saturation, the faster the Original recovers from hibernation
- **The long-term truth**: even without Spera, weathering of the prison and the slow seepage of pure magic through its imperfections would wake the Original eventually — but on a timescale of further millennia. Spera's corruption collapses that timescale to years
- The only practical accelerant is industrial-scale violet processing, which leaks corruption into the atmosphere with every end-use device. Ordinary magic use (mages casting, ancient artifacts being powered) consumes ambient magic rather than releasing it, and so does not contribute to the awakening
- **Awakening and release are distinct — growth is the door**: waking does not free it. What frees it is what its body does once metabolism resumes: it grows. The Original is physically indestructible; the prison is merely hard. An expanding, unkillable mass inside a crystal shell ends one way — like roots splitting stone, slowly at first, then in accelerating fractures, the formation crumbles from within. Growth requires pure magic to fuel it: the trickle seeping through the prison's imperfections keeps pre-breach growth glacial (which is why the no-Spera timescale is further millennia), but a mining breach changes everything — open ambient magic floods in, the Original can finally feed, and a fed Original cannot be contained by crystal. The breach is therefore not just an awakening risk but the release vector itself
- It is not conscious and holds no grudge. It wakes hungry, and it grows
- Once free, it will feed on ambient pure magic and produce new spores, beginning a new cycle of exponential corruption

**Timeline**: The discovery of the largest deposit and subsequent mining will occur during the story. The player does not know about the Original or the threat at the start - they will uncover this truth gradually.

## Ancient History

### The Height of Civilization

- Far more advanced than the current era
- Magic was deeply integrated into society
- Technology and capabilities that seem miraculous to modern people
- Quality of life enhanced by abundant magic

### The Arrival and Infestation

- Meteorite brought the Original organism
- It fed, grew, released spores
- Spores spread, each one feeding and releasing more spores
- Corruption spread across large portions of the planet
- By the time the disastrous impact of the organism was discovered, it was already too late - the spores had already spread globally
- Local containment was no longer possible

### Attempts to Stop It

- The ancients tried to kill the spores
- It was possible but required effort and knowledge
- They could not keep up with the spread rate
- They attempted to destroy the Original - nothing worked (indestructible)

**The Cost to Mages:**
- Mages who attempted to destroy the Original or its spores paid a terrible price
- Casting spells in corruption-saturated areas required absorbing that corrupted magic directly into their bodies
- The first mages to confront the Original directly were unaware of this danger — they survived long enough to report back, but died in the days or weeks that followed
- Fighting the infestation was effectively suicide — the people most capable of stopping it were the most vulnerable to it

### The Decision to Seal

**Testing**: The ancients tested on captured spores and confirmed they died without access to magic. This was an informed decision.

**The Delay**: People could not resolve themselves to something so drastic. They spent time looking for a better resolution. During this delay, the civilization continued to decline as corruption spread further.

**Limited Time**: The Original and its spores produced corrupted magic far faster than what occurs with modern violet crystal processing. From meteor impact to the Death of Magic was approximately 2 years. There was not much time to prepare for the aftermath, but the ancients had teleportation, magical communication, and emergency logistics that allowed mass coordination on a timescale impossible in the modern medieval era.

**The Choice**: They knew sealing all magic would cause catastrophe - collapse of their civilization, mass death, a dark age. But there was no other option. The more time passed, the more magic became corrupted and the more civilization suffered. If left to spread further, no one would have survived.

**Multi-Nation Agreement**: The decision was made in agreement with most nations of that era — 5 nations signed the post-Death of Magic pact and agreed on 6 specialized roles for the rebuilding of civilization that would follow. Possibly not all nations of the era agreed. The 6th role (the Keepers) was assigned to a population that did not yet exist as a nation: it would be constructed at the seal-casting hub from the mages who remained behind to cast the spell, plus the otherworlders who stayed with them. See Pact Nation Roles below for the full breakdown.

**The Fate of the Pact Nations**: Most of the original pact nations no longer exist as such. Over the millennia following the Death of Magic, wars, plagues, famine, and the general brutality of the dark age dissolved or transformed most of them. The pact nations were not naive about war - warfare existed in the ancient era - but they did not anticipate that the catastrophe itself would generate the conditions for it. The trauma, hunger, and desperation of the dark age produced conflicts of a scale and ferocity they had no framework to prepare for. Today only a handful of successor states remain, and smaller nations have formed since, some descended from pact fragments, others arising independently.

### Pact Nation Roles

**The Logic of Specialization**: The ancients knew that after the Death of Magic, communication and travel between nations would be extremely difficult — no teleportation, weakened animals, collapsed infrastructure. No single nation could master everything needed to rebuild civilization. Each nation was assigned a specialized role matching the geographic advantages of its own existing territory — the nations did not relocate. (The post-seal evacuation moved *people* — above all the summoned otherworlders, plus displaced populations — to nations that stayed where they were.) Each would develop deep expertise in one domain and share it with the others.

**The Confluence (10-Year Festival)**: To share knowledge between nations, the pact established a gathering every 10 years — known as **The Confluence**. The name evokes rivers from different sources flowing together: each nation brought its specialized knowledge, mixed it with the others, and carried home what the others had developed. It was a massive festival where each nation presented the advancements they had made in their assigned domain. This was not just celebration; it was load-bearing infrastructure for civilization's survival. Techniques, discoveries, and innovations developed by one nation were disseminated to all.

**Location and Organization**: The Confluence was held at a site in the central region of the continent, deliberately chosen so that no nation had a hostile-distance disadvantage in attending. Organization was handled by a standing council of six representatives — one from each pact nation — who coordinated logistics, agendas, and the safe passage of delegations between festivals. Communication during the post-DoM era was difficult, not impossible: messengers traveled by river and road carrying physical letters, and the council's continued existence between festivals was itself one of the pact era's quiet achievements.

**The End**: The Confluence had been weakening for generations before its end — younger generations in the other pact nations had grown indifferent or hostile to the sharing principle, attendance had thinned, and commitment had eroded. Vaeren's invasion of Selvaren (see Vaeren → Why the Shield Turned) was the final shock: when one pact nation destroyed another, the institution lost its premise. The council could not function with a Selvaren seat that no longer existed and a Shield seat occupied by the destroyer. The southwestern fragments consolidated into Aldemar in the same generation, defining the political shape of the dark age that followed. Knowledge transfer stopped with the institution: each surviving nation kept only what it had specialized in, and lost access to the rest.

**The Six Roles**:

| Role | Ancient Name | Specialization | Geographic Basis |
|------|------|---------------|-----------------|
| **The Sowers** | **Maerith** | Agriculture, food production, animal husbandry | Most fertile plains and valleys |
| **The Menders** | **Selvaren** | Medicine, healing, managing magic withdrawal | Region rich in medicinal flora and resources |
| **The Shapers** | **Kaldun** | Construction, tools, metallurgy, engineering | Mineral-rich, mountainous terrain |
| **The Shields** | **Vaeren** | Underground creature containment, fortification, combat techniques | Densest concentration of underground cave exits |
| **The Keepers** | **Iyenor** | Knowledge preservation, archiving history and ancient records | The northern island, chosen as the seal-casters' post-seal destination for isolation and long-term safety |
| **The Mariners** | **Aolanir** | Shipbuilding, navigation, fishing, sea transport | Forested coastline with natural harbors |

**Why These Roles**: Each maps to a critical survival need that could be geographically concentrated. The Sowers needed the best farmland. The Shapers needed mineral deposits. The Shields needed to be where the underground exits were densest. The Mariners needed coastline and timber. The Menders needed access to medicinal resources. The Keepers' role and destination were planned together (see The Keepers' Special Case below).

**The Keepers' Special Case**: Unlike the other five roles, the Keepers were not assigned to an existing population in their pre-seal territory. The pact planners designed the role and the destination together: the ~100 most competent mages who would remain at the hub to cast the seal, plus the few hundred otherworlders who stayed with them, would migrate to the northern island after the spell took effect. The island had been prepared in advance — the most important ancient archives were teleported there before the seal, while teleportation still worked, because moving large archives by river and foot in a post-magic world would have been brutally impractical. The migrants would arrive to find the records already in place, and would develop, over generations, into a culture of preservation. None of these mages or otherworlders were "Keepers" before the assignment — the role and the unified identity crystallized post-seal among a deliberately mixed population. The island was chosen for its natural isolation, which made it an ideal long-term refuge for the archives during the dark age that would follow.

**The Mariners' Critical Function**: With overland travel crippled (weakened animals, no magical transport), sea and river routes became the primary means of moving large quantities of goods between nations. The Mariners' ships carried the Shapers' metal, the Sowers' grain, and the delegates to each festival. They were arguably the connective tissue of the pact.

**Role Survival in the Modern Era**: Most pact roles have been forgotten or corrupted beyond recognition over the millennia. Some nations retain cultural echoes of their original purpose without understanding the origin. The mapping of ancient roles to modern nations is still being determined (see Nations section).

### The Ancient Teleportation Network

**City Terminals**: The Ancients built large teleportation structures in their greatest and most important cities. They were major pieces of infrastructure rather than something found in every settlement, so even when restored they do not make ordinary travel obsolete.

**Hub-Routed Travel**: Every terminal routed its travelers through the central mountain hub. A traveler entered a city terminal, arrived at the hub, then selected an onward destination. In the modern restoration of the network, activated destinations can be selected while inactive ones remain visible but greyed out. The player therefore revisits the hub whenever using an Ancient teleporter.

**The Outbound Principle**: The network contains the smaller-scale conceptual basis for sending something away from its point of origin. Its spatial and directional principles will eventually become the foundation of modern research into outbound dimensional travel, although travel between worlds requires vastly greater scale, precision, and power than travel between cities.

**Modern Restoration**: Not all surviving terminals have been restored. Vaeren's researchers have successfully reactivated some within their own country, likely because their work around the Hollows led them toward expertise in exits, thresholds, spatial boundaries, anchoring, and related forms of dimensional magic.

**Player Progression**: By working with Vaeren's researchers, the player can learn how to reactivate compatible terminals encountered across Aldemar and the rest of the world. This is a potential side-quest line that gradually expands the fast-travel network. Exact restoration requirements remain TBD.

### The Teleportation Hub

**Original Purpose**: A central facility in the mountains, built as the routing heart of inter-city teleportation. Its remote isolation allowed the extraordinary magical demands of the shared hub to draw on the area's ambient magic without competing with a city's countless other uses. Individual terminals could therefore stand in the greatest Ancient cities while the network's central burden remained concentrated here.

**Conversion for the Pact**: When the decision to seal was made, most teleportation devices at the hub were converted into otherworlder summoning portals, to bring as many otherworlders as possible before the seal. The conversion was possible because summoning is a derivative of teleportation — an adaptation of the same mature discipline, not a new technology invented from nothing in the crisis window. Some teleportation devices were kept operational for the evacuation - moving populations to their assigned pact nations.

**Why Centralization Was Essential**:
- **Skill distribution**: All otherworlders arrived at the same place, allowing the ancients to assess their skills and compose balanced groups
- Although same-planet groups were kept together, distribution to nations was done with utility in mind
- Each group sent to a nation was carefully composed to ensure they had enough of each type of knowledge (farmers, healers, builders, etc.)
- If each nation had summoned separately, they would have received random skill distributions — potentially disastrous for survival

**Creating a Safe Zone**:
- The site required a coordinated effort by all nations to make it safe for mages to work. Spores can be destroyed safely with care, but in most of the world they pop up faster than any local force can clear them — clearance loses to spawn rate, and the area degrades again
- The pact concentrated its best mages at the hub specifically to break that ratio. With enough top-tier casters, careful protocols, and time, the zone was fully cleared without losses, and then kept clear through constant monitoring for the duration of the operation
- This allowed mages to build and operate the devices in a stable, corruption-free pocket — something that could not be sustained at any other site, where the spawn rate would have outpaced the clearance work long before the summoning could be completed

**Why Proximity Still Mattered**:
- All living things experienced drawbacks from the lack of magic — not just humans
- Animals bred faster than humans, so new generations without withdrawal effects emerged more quickly
- However, during the immediate aftermath, horses and other beasts of burden were affected and could not carry loads as they would have in the past
- This made long-distance travel difficult, which is why even a central location was better than building devices at the edges of the known world

**The Casting of the Seal**: After populations were evacuated to their designated nations, the Death of Magic spell was cast at the hub. Approximately 100 of the most competent mages remained to perform the spell. A few hundred otherworlders stayed with them - not to help cast, but to help the mages survive the immediate aftermath, as the mages would be struck by magic withdrawal the moment the spell took effect. After the spell was cast, the otherworlders helped the weakened mages descend by river through the mountains to the northern coast, then crossed the sea to the northern island. The full route — river, coastal staging, sea crossing — had been planned and provisioned in advance, with boats and supplies pre-positioned along the way, since post-seal travel logistics could not be improvised.

**The Northern Island Group**: The mages and otherworlders who retreated to the northern island were unique — they were the only group that had not been teleported to their designated nation. They traveled on foot and by river, which meant they were the only people who knew the physical route to the hub and the locations of the summoning devices. This group's otherworlders were the ones who, 5-10 years later, found and destroyed the summoning devices (see The Summoning Devices below). Their knowledge of the hub's location was exclusive, and their descendants form modern Iyenor (the Keepers Nation — see Iyenor under Nations).

**Current State**: Most of the hub is now an overgrown, partially collapsed ruin at the center of the mountain range. Its transit chamber and routing function nevertheless remain usable wherever a restored city terminal reconnects to them: a traveler arrives at the hub and chooses among the currently activated destinations, while most remain inactive. Exactly how the central routing system survived, was reactivated, and is powered remains TBD.

**The Graveyard of Summoning Devices**: The player first sees the remains of the Ancients' mass-converted teleporters here: a graveyard of destroyed devices resembling the surviving Caelvenor prototype. These are the devices the northern-island otherworlders destroyed after the Death of Magic. Many are broken, vegetation-covered, or partly dismantled by later visitors. Their resemblance reveals that the Caelvenor machine belonged to a much larger technological family and is an exceptional survivor.

### The Death of Magic

- Through means now lost, they crystallized ALL magic on the planet
- Pure magic became blue crystals
- Corrupted magic became violet crystals
- Magic did not crystallize uniformly across the landscape: it condensed at concentration points — veins, pockets, nodes — the way dew condenses on surfaces rather than evenly through the air. The result was deposits, not a planet-wide crust. The densest deposits formed where magic itself was densest: in the deep underground (see Blue Crystals → The deep reserves) and, for violet, around the centers of corruption
- All ambient magic vanished
- The spores died (confirmed by prior testing)
- The Original did not die - it entered hibernation

### The Aftermath

- Everything built on magic stopped working
- Infrastructure collapsed
- Famine, disease, social collapse
- Mass death
- Humanity survived but was thrown back to primitive conditions
- Most knowledge was lost
- Thousands of years of slow rebuilding to reach current medieval level

### Magic Withdrawal

The population of the ancient era had absorbed magic their entire lives. When magic vanished, their bodies suffered withdrawal.

**Symptoms:**
- Physical weakness and fatigue
- Mental fog, difficulty concentrating
- Depression and emotional instability
- Chronic pain or discomfort
- More prone to sickness, with more severe symptoms
- Shortened lifespan in severe cases

**Severity**: Most people were not completely debilitated, but they were diminished - easily tired, more vulnerable to illness, bodies struggling to function without magic.

**Duration**: A single generation. Those who experienced the Death of Magic directly took a few years to a decade for their bodies to accommodate and learn to live without magic. Children born after the seal did not experience withdrawal.

**Why Otherworlders Were Essential**: Summoned people from non-magical worlds had never absorbed magic. They did not experience withdrawal. While the native population struggled with diminished capacity, otherworlders were fully functional - healthy workers who could help maintain society during the crisis.

### Lost Knowledge and Archives

**What Remains**: Ancient ruins stand as testament to the old civilization's might. Archives and records exist in various locations.

**Non-Magical Writings**: Some non-magical writings were left in people's hands after the Death of Magic, and some were written afterward. However, most have been lost to time, and those that remain cannot be read due to the language barrier.

**Why It's Inaccessible**: Ancient knowledge is double-locked:
1. **Language barrier**: The Ancient language has been lost to time. No one can read the texts.
2. **Magic barrier**: Archives require active magic input to open — the seals are mechanisms that only respond to deliberate magical manipulation, not passive barriers. The bar is low: opening one takes pushing a modest amount of mana into the mechanism, not advanced casting. The language, not the magic, is the true lock.

**Why the Archives Were Magic-Locked at All**: The archives were not built for the catastrophe — nothing was; there was no time. They are the old civilization's ordinary repositories: assembled long before the crisis, holding documents that were often magical objects themselves, behind seals that were standard institutional practice in a world saturated with magic. When the seal decision came, preservation was triaged, not redesigned: the most important collections were teleported whole to Iyenor, and everything else stayed where it stood, locked as it had always been. Some among the planners saw the flaw — knowledge sealed behind magic, about to be orphaned in a magicless world — and pushed for durable, non-magical preservation, but copying entire archives by hand in under two years, during a civilizational collapse, was impossible. What that concern produced instead was partial: the non-magical writings made before and especially after the seal as a means to remember (most proved too fragile for the dark age, though some survive in the ruins of nations and can still be found — see Archive Fragments), and, generations later, Iyenor's monolith project (see The Iyenor Monoliths), which solved durability and accessibility at once — stone, readable without magic — only to be defeated in turn by the death of the language carved into it.

**Why the Language Was Lost**: The otherworlders summoned during the Death of Magic were taught the Ancient language through the summoning process, but they still spoke their own native languages. Otherworlders came from different non-magical worlds, though not a huge number of worlds — some groups were summoned from the same world and stayed together.

These people were unwillingly summoned to another world. They could not return home — without magic, the summoning devices were useless. Preserving their language and culture was the least they could do. Since going back was impossible, they opted for the second-best thing: making this place feel like home.

Before the Death of Magic, both summoning and ancient magic supported universal language comprehension. After the seal, no new otherworlders arrived; the magic-based translation the *natives* had relied on (spells and artifacts) vanished with ambient magic, while the summoned otherworlders kept their imbued comprehension for life (see Otherworlder Abilities — a permanent rewiring, not a sustained spell). Eventually the last universally gifted generations died. Ancient did not disappear immediately. It survived for generations, especially among mages, scholars, and Keepers, but it gradually became less useful than the living languages spoken by mixed native-otherworlder populations.

Since many natives were weakened by magic withdrawal, otherworlders became some of the most important and influential people of their time. Over generations, the true Ancient language was modified beyond recognition and eventually lost — competing with multiple otherworlder languages, and lacking practical use (the archives were inaccessible anyway). Over centuries and then millennia, it became like Latin without a living priesthood strong enough to preserve fluency: studied in fragments, quoted in rituals, visible in old texts, but no longer truly understood. For why fluency died even on Iyenor — the one nation dedicated to preservation — see Iyenor (The Keepers Nation) → What They Lost.

**The Dark Centuries**: In the centuries immediately following the Death of Magic, archives were completely inaccessible — there was literally no magic to work with. Even if someone could somehow read the language, no one could use magic to open the sealed archives. This period lasted centuries, possibly a millennium.

**Modern Attempts**: As magic gradually returned, some mages managed to open archives over the centuries. However, many archives have been damaged or lost to time — millennia of neglect, wars, natural decay, and deliberate destruction during the dark age took their toll. Scholars have been studying the Ancient language, and some words or context can now be understood at a surface level. However, this understanding remains too fragmentary to extract meaningful knowledge from complex texts.

**Current Understanding**: People know a great civilization existed and fell. They do not know how or why. Various theories exist, but nothing is certain. The truth — scattered across surviving archives that can now be physically opened but not read — awaits someone who can understand the Ancient language.

### Knowledge Degradation Over Time

**Ancient Era**: People understood the distinction between pure and corrupted magic. They knew blue crystals were crystallized pure magic and violet crystals were crystallized corrupted magic.

**Post-Death of Magic**: With no ambient magic, no one could use either type of crystal. The practical distinction became irrelevant — both were inert, useless stones.

**Over Millennia**: Without practical relevance, the "why" behind the distinction was forgotten. Only vague warnings survived. Written records were mostly lost — they were in the Ancient language, and no one saw practical importance in preserving warnings about stones they couldn't use anyway. The first generations after the Death of Magic may have cared, but subsequent generations did not, and the knowledge faded.

**Modern Understanding**: Before the processing breakthrough, people simply thought violet crystals were a variant of blue crystals that they hadn't figured out how to use yet. The two look similar enough that this assumption was reasonable.

### The Iyenor Monoliths

Faced with the slow erosion of paper archives, the loss of fluent Ancient speakers, and the geographic fragility of any single repository, the Keepers of Iyenor proposed during the Confluence era a more durable form of preservation: **stone monoliths inscribed in Ancient**, erected at sites across the world so that critical knowledge would exist in many places at once and could not be lost to a single fire, war, or shipwreck.

**Why Stone**: Paper degrades, archives can be sacked, and the island itself was a single point of failure. Stone outlasts civilizations. Even if no one alive could read what was carved, the carvings would still be there centuries later, waiting.

**Distribution**: Monoliths were placed in many nations during the centuries the Confluence still functioned. Iyenor sent emissaries who traveled with the carvers, selected sites, taught local custodians what little they could about the importance of the markers, and then moved on. This was a generations-long undertaking — not a single project but a continuous diplomatic and logistical effort sustained as long as the pact era held together.

**Modern State**: Most monoliths are gone. War, deliberate destruction, weathering, and the simple fact that nobody could read them and many came to view them as pagan oddities all took their toll. Some were repurposed as building stone. Some were defaced. A handful survive, in varying conditions — some intact and legible, some half-buried, some broken into fragments scattered across a ruin.

**Why They Matter Now**: A surviving monolith is one of the few places a modern person can encounter Ancient text in the wild, outside the sealed archives of Iyenor. For the player — who can read Ancient — each surviving monolith is a recoverable fragment of lost knowledge, encountered as part of exploring the world rather than requiring a journey to the island.

### Names and Naming (Ancient Era)

Names from the pre-Death of Magic civilization draw from a broad palette unique to that culture. They are rooted in a language lineage that existed before any otherworlder arrived on the planet and do not map to any real Earth naming tradition.

**Shared qualities across the palette:**
- Old, weathered, culturally rooted
- Distinctly non-Earth in feel — the reader should not instinctively reach for "French," "Greek," "Hebrew," or any other recognizable real-world tradition
- Evocative of a civilization that lasted over a millennium, allowing for regional and generational variation

**Stylistic directions that all fit within this palette:**
- **Soft & flowing**: Vaeren, Sellis, Iyen, Ohara
- **Rooted & consonant-rich**: Therek, Kaldun, Orvan, Maerith
- **Vowel-heavy, old-feeling**: Aolan, Iora, Ulya, Evael

Any of these styles can appear. A civilization spanning ~1024 years would have multiple regional variations, historical fashions, and generational shifts.

**Modern names** (used in the current era) have a different aesthetic — they descend from otherworlder languages that arrived around the time of the Death of Magic, drifted through roughly 2,300 years of linguistic evolution. Importantly, otherworlders were summoned from **multiple non-magical worlds, not only Earth**, and some of those worlds may not have been inhabited by humans at all. This means the modern naming pool is genuinely heterogeneous:

- Some names descend from Earth languages of ~2,300 years ago and can feel half-familiar (names like Marek, Iula, Theris) without being exact matches
- Other names descend from languages of other non-magical human worlds — similar register to Earth names but not traceable to any real culture
- A portion may descend from non-human otherworlder languages, producing names that feel genuinely foreign to any Earth ear

All of these have been churning together for ~2,300 years of mixing, drift, and evolution. Modern naming conventions will be developed in more detail separately.

**Names do not carry across the two eras.** Ancient names and modern names belong to separate lineages; any resemblance between an ancient name and a modern one is a rare coincidence of drift, not continuity. (Vaeren retaining its ancient name as a *nation* is the deliberate, conspicuous exception — see Vaeren.)

**Note**: The existence of non-human otherworlders has implications beyond naming (modern population ancestry, visible demographics, cultural memory). Flagged in Open Questions for later resolution.

### Surviving Oral Tradition

Despite the loss of written knowledge, some memories survived through oral tradition - though warped by thousands of years of retelling.

**What Death of Magic Era Survivors Tried to Preserve:**
- A terrible monster destroyed the old world
- The monster's lair became covered in violet crystal after the catastrophe
- Violet crystals appeared in places where corruption was worst
- Violet was linked to corruption and to the monster

**What Actually Survived (Modern Era):**
- The specific details above degraded into vague, childlike tales over millennia
- No actionable warnings remain - just fuzzy folklore
- The color violet is associated with bad luck, ill omens, or misfortune
- Stories to frighten children, not serious historical knowledge
- No one knows WHERE the monster's lair was, or what specifically to avoid
- Most educated people consider these stories primitive myths with no factual basis

**How Warnings Were Dismissed:**
- When early violet crystal processing experiments showed no immediate harm, scholars dismissed the old warnings as baseless superstition
- The gradual accumulation of harm let people rationalize: "If it were truly dangerous, we'd have seen it immediately"
- By the time the warnings proved true, the neighboring nation was already dependent on violet crystal energy

### The Underground Threat — The Hollows

**Name**: The creatures that emerge from the underground are collectively known as **The Hollows**. The name has been worn smooth by millennia of use — its original meaning is debated and largely forgotten. It evokes multiple things at once: the hollow places they come from (caves, depressions, the empty spaces beneath the earth), the unsettling sense that something is *missing* from them in a way no one can quite articulate, and the resonant, echoing sounds they make moving through stone tunnels. People in the Shield nation simply call them the Hollows the way one names something too familiar to embellish.

**Nature**: Beneath the continent lies an extensive network of deep cave systems harboring an entire subterranean ecosystem. Magic density increases with depth — the closer to the planet's core, the denser the ambient magic. As a result, creatures dwelling deeper underground are more magical and more dangerous, while those near the surface are largely non-magical and less threatening. The underground is a gradient from mundane to extraordinarily dangerous.

**The Two Magics of the Vertical World**: Magic regenerates from the planet's core outward, while corruption is released by surface devices and accumulates in the atmosphere. The deep therefore refills with the purest magic on the planet, and the surface is where the corrupted mix lives. Deep Hollows and surface life are bathing in literally different magic — which is why rising deep magic empowers the underground ecosystem at the same time as trace surface corruption sickens ordinary, weakly-magical animals (see Corrupted Magic → How Harm Scales — the Saturation Rule). By the same rule, a surfaced Hollow lingering in a heavily corrupted region would itself slowly sicken — negligible at today's trace levels outside Spera, but a real effect at high concentrations.

**Ancient Management**: In ancient times, mages sealed all dangerous exits from the underground — the deep access points where powerful, magical creatures could surface. However, some entrances remained open and regulated, used for hunting and gathering rare materials from the creatures within — the work of **Hollow hunters** (also called delvers). Venturing into the upper levels of the caves was a known, managed profession — dangerous but profitable (see Hollow Hunters below).

**Post-Death of Magic**: When magic vanished, the magical wards on sealed exits broke. The deepest, most magic-dependent creatures did not die. Deprived of the dense ambient magic their bodies depend on (see Magic System → Highly Magical Life), many entered a state of deep slumber or biological suspension. The non-magical and less magic-dependent creatures of the upper and mid levels remained active. With the wards gone, these creatures began surfacing freely through exits across the continent.

**Why They Cannot Be Eradicated**: The cave systems extend deeper than anyone can explore. The subterranean ecosystem is vast and self-sustaining. Humans can only manage the surface exits — they cannot invade the underground world and eliminate the source population. The creatures surface for food, territory, and breeding cycles. It is persistent animal behavior — endless, like tides.

**Why They Leave the Underground**: The underground is home — the creatures do not abandon it casually. (What follows is the *mostly-agreed* model among those who study the Hollows, not settled fact; the deep is unexplored and much remains unknown.)
- **The enabler — the magic gradient**: Magic is densest at the planet's core and thins toward the surface. The deep creatures are adapted to *high* magic, so historically the magic-poor surface was hostile to them — the powerful ones physically could not range up, and only the weakest, least magic-dependent creatures near the top could tolerate the thin upper layers. Crucially, magic returns *from the core outward*, so the **deep refills first**: this both **wakes the dormant apex creatures** and **flattens the gradient**, making the surface tolerable to creatures that could never have survived up here before. The barrier that caged the dangerous ones below is eroding — which is why Hollow activity is worsening now (and why Spera's atmospheric corruption quietly feeds the same problem that feeds the Original's awakening: corrupted magic adds to the *surface-level* total of ambient magic, flattening the gradient from the top while the planet's pure production refills it from below — see The Two Magics of the Vertical World)
- **The routine driver — hunger and overpopulation**: Even setting magic aside, the weak upper-level creatures forage upward when food or territory runs short below. Constant, opportunistic, low-level — the background trickle (see Modern Hollow Breaks → the routine leak)
- **The acute driver — a waking giant**: When a powerful creature (or several) stirs in the depths and begins moving, it disrupts everything above it. The smaller creatures flee the apex predator in a stampede pushed up toward the exits and out — a waking giant flushes a whole column of fleeing creatures ahead of it. That pressure wave reaching the surface *is* a Hollow Break (see Modern Hollow Breaks)

**Distribution**: Every nation has some cave exits to manage, but one region sits atop the densest concentration — essentially the roof of the largest underground network. This became the basis for the Shield nation's pact role (see Pact Nation Roles).

**Modern Era**: As magic has slowly returned (~50% of ancient levels), the underground ecosystem is shifting again. Deeper creatures that were dormant or weakened are regaining strength. Some mages have helped re-seal certain exits, but the threat remains very much present. The underground gradient of magical density means that as ambient magic continues to rise, increasingly dangerous creatures may begin surfacing again.

**Hollow Hunters**: In the ancient era, venturing into the regulated upper caves to bring back precious magical materials was an established profession — **Hollow hunters** (delvers) who went *voluntarily* and deep, for profit. The practice all but died with the Death of Magic. Over the past few centuries it has slowly crept back: adventurers once again descend into certain known, relatively stable Hollow caves to fetch magical ingredients and, where they can reach them, veins of high-purity blue crystal — the deep deposits are the richest left on the planet (see The Crystals → Blue Crystals). It remains a **rare** calling — dangerous and specialized — and is **mostly Vaereni by origin**: as descendants of the Shield nation, Vaereni carry the deepest cultural fluency with the Hollows and the most inherited experience facing them. (This overlaps with the mercenary trade — the Vaereni specialists hired to fight Breaks come from the same cultural well; see Vaeren. It is also the in-world frame for the player's own cave delving.)

### Modern Hollow Breaks

**The baseline — the routine leak**: Hollows slip out of the underground all the time. The overwhelming majority of this is low-level: weak creatures driven up by hunger or crowding (see The Hollows → Why They Leave) that wander through minor exits and are dealt with by soldiers on routine patrol. This is the ordinary background state of the world, not an emergency — and it is *not* called a Break. Because the state guards and manages most trade routes, travelers rarely meet danger on the established roads; it is mostly when leaving the used ways that one encounters roaming Hollows. **Most** of the monsters roaming the world originate from the Hollows this way — but not all: some ordinary creatures (rats and the like) are simply natural fauna, and the player will meet a few of these too.

**What a "Hollow Break" actually means**: The term is reserved for serious incidents — when the Hollows emerging are too numerous or too powerful for the locals to contain alone, requiring intervention from neighboring cities, hired specialists (such as Vaeren mercenaries), or more. The trigger is typically a powerful creature waking deep down and flushing a flight-cascade of smaller creatures up and out (see The Hollows → Why They Leave). Breaks have occurred throughout the centuries since the Death of Magic; containing the worst of them was the original basis of the Shield role — Vaeren has dealt with them continuously since the wards failed, and other nations have faced their own across the millennia. When a Break is put down, its most dangerous creatures are killed, but it is never *fully* eradicated — the routine leak simply resumes.

**What is changing now**: As ambient magic levels continue to rise, the underground ecosystem is stirring more broadly. Breaks are becoming more frequent and more severe, and deeper, more ancient Hollows that had been dormant or weakened during the magic-poor centuries are beginning to awaken. Some are bold enough to surface through exits that had been quiet for generations — including exits in nations like Aldemar that historically saw little Hollow activity. For most of Aldemar's existence, the truly dangerous Breaks were concentrated around the Shield lands; Aldemar's own incidents were sparse and modest. The last few decades have changed that, as rising magic — and lately Spera's corrupted contribution to the surface total (see The Two Magics of the Vertical World) — stirs the deep: the Break that struck Aldemar seventeen years ago (see Aelis) and the current Aldemar Break are the two landmark cases, with smaller incidents scattered between them.

**The Aldemar Break**: Approximately one year before the player's arrival, a Hollow Break began within Aldemar. It is not a single eruption but a sustained upwelling: the deep disturbance keeps flushing creatures toward the surface, so the affected zone festers and creeps rather than bursting once and subsiding — and Aldemar's lack of anti-Hollow expertise (see below) is why it festers instead of being lanced. The affected zone has been progressively abandoned as it grows month by month. The cause is consistent with the broader pattern — rising magic stirring deeper, bolder Hollows in numbers Aldemar had never needed to manage.

**Why the Response Has Been Inadequate**: Aldemar historically had relatively few Hollow exits, so it never needed to maintain serious anti-Hollow expertise. Knowledge that once flowed through the Confluence has decayed in places that didn't face the threat regularly. They are now learning on the job, with fragmented institutional memory. Adding insult to injury: the nation that retained the deepest Hollow expertise — Vaeren — has cold, centuries-old enmity with Aldemar, making cross-border knowledge requests politically fraught. (Hiring *individual* Vaeren mercenaries — Hollow-fighting specialists who sell their skills for coin — is a separate matter and remains possible even where official state-to-state cooperation is frozen; see Current State and Act 1 — Find a Way Back Home.)

**Current State**:
- The inhabitable zone is still growing, month over month
- Refugees displaced from the affected region have arrived in surrounding areas, including the village where the player begins
- The crown has moved to address the situation more aggressively — hiring **Vaeren mercenaries** (Hollow-fighting specialists) and drawing on the participation of neighboring cities
- Soldiers and mages visibly travel through the village toward the affected area — heading to the front, returning from it, or coordinating en route

## The Dark Age

The period immediately following the Death of Magic — characterized by collapse, survival, and the eventual rebuilding of civilization.

### Otherworlder-Native Relations

**The Guilt of the Ancients**: The ancient mages and elders were acutely aware of what they had inflicted on the summoned people. They had forcibly brought strangers to a dying world. This guilt shaped every interaction.

**How Natives Treated Otherworlders**:
- They treated otherworlders with kindness
- They explained everything about what had happened and why
- They apologized throughout — to those summoned early in the crisis and to those who arrived in the final days before the seal — and they kept apologizing afterward, through the years when the natives had become powerless and frail. (No one was summoned *after* the seal itself: without magic, the devices were dead)
- The ancient mages taught everything they could to the otherworlders

**How Otherworlders Responded**:
- Most felt resentment — this was natural and expected
- With time and the kindness shown by the elders, they managed to get by
- Some even came to appreciate their new lives
- Those who still felt hatred generally did not show it or act on it — they understood their predicament
- Rare cases of outward hostility were dealt with by their peers (other otherworlders)

**Division of Roles**:
- Otherworlders were the "arms" of this new era — the healthy workforce that could function without magic
- Ancient mages were the "brains" — they possessed the knowledge and taught it to the otherworlders

### Organization of Otherworlders

**Origin Worlds**: Otherworlders were summoned from multiple non-magical worlds. Not all of those worlds were inhabited by humans — some otherworlders came from non-human civilizations. The summoning devices targeted non-magical realities, not specifically humanoid ones, so the arriving groups included intelligent beings of various kinds. The ancient mages worked with whoever arrived, regardless of species.

**Descendants Today**: The descendants of non-human otherworlders still exist in the modern era. They are part of the world's normal population — not hidden, not dying out. Specific details about how many distinct species remain, how they integrate with human populations, and how much is culturally remembered about their otherworlder origin are still TBD.

**Keeping Origin Groups Together**: The ancient mages, out of guilt and humanity (and, for non-human groups, whatever the equivalent sentiment was), made it possible for people from the same origin world to remain together. This was a deliberate kindness.

**Assignment to Nations**:
- Groups of otherworlders (thousands to tens of thousands) were assigned to different nations
- Each group was accompanied by mages from that nation
- The nations of that era are not the same as current nations

**Pre-Death Planning**:
- Before the Death of Magic, heads of nations reunited to plan for the aftermath
- Each nation was assigned a specific role in the rebuilding (see Pact Nation Roles section under Ancient History for the full list of six roles)
- The Keepers were assigned the role of "Those Who Remember" — their duty was to preserve knowledge and prevent future catastrophes of this scale. Unlike the other roles, this one was uniquely constructed at the seal-casting hub rather than tied to an existing nation; their descendants form modern Iyenor (see The Keepers' Special Case under Pact Nation Roles)

### The Collapse

Despite the planning and cooperation, the situation was catastrophic.

**The Scale of Death**:
- Millions died
- When otherworlders reached their respective nations, they found panic, desperation, sickness, and famine
- There was not enough food for everyone
- Food production would take time to ramp up

**Wars for Resources**:
- The six pact nations held together: the Confluence functioned for roughly five centuries, and no pact nation turned on another until Vaeren's invasion of Selvaren (Modern Year 536; see Vaeren → Why the Shield Turned)
- Outside the pact, the picture was darker: nations that had not signed, peripheral polities, and breakaway territories waged desperate wars for food and resources. Some of these wars were led by otherworlders
- Several non-pact nations fell entirely during these early conflicts
- Internal strife also flared within pact nations — riots, secessions, local collapses — without breaking the pact itself

**What Was Lost**:
- No pact role was erased outright, but most were hollowed, fused, or reduced to habit — the *system* of specialization-plus-sharing died even where individual knowledge survived:
  - **Shield** (Vaeren): survived intact — the only role still consciously practiced
  - **Mender** (Selvaren): died with the nation (~Modern Year 500); fragments of medical knowledge scattered into the Aldemar fusion, but the institutional depth was lost
  - **Sower, Shaper, Mariner** (Maerith, Kaldun, Aolanir): fused and blurred inside Aldemar — much of the practical knowledge persists, but the specialized identities and the sharing mandate dissolved in the fusion
  - **Keeper** (Iyenor): persists as ritual without comprehension — they guard what they can no longer read
- Ancient nations do not map to current nations — the political landscape was reshaped across this era and the centuries that followed

**Governments**:
- Governments survived the Death of Magic initially
- However, all nations' governments underwent significant changes following the integration of otherworlders and the pressures of survival

## The Crystals

### Using Crystals

- Using crystals to power anything requires ambient magic to exist
- Crystals cannot be used in isolation - there must be magic in the environment to interface with them
- In the centuries immediately following the Death of Magic, crystals were useless - there was no ambient magic to work with
- This is why the ancients could not use crystallized magic to sustain their economy after the seal
- **Raw crystals are inert.** Both blue and violet crystals must be mined AND processed into a usable form before any device can draw on them. Processing requires specialized facilities and is costly — this is what makes crystal-powered devices a luxury rather than a commodity in most of the world
- Crystal-powered devices fall into two broad categories:
  - **Crafted artifacts with fixed functions** — lanterns that light without oil, healers' diagnostic instruments, enchanted blades, sealed lockboxes. These let non-mages access magical effects through preset, single-purpose mechanisms
  - **Mage batteries** — artifacts designed to amplify a mage's potency. The mage absorbs energy from the battery (drawn from the crystal feeding it) and channels it through their own casting, effectively expanding their mana pool. A sufficiently charged battery also lets a mage feed extra energy into ancient artifacts that no single modern mage could power on their own (see Ancient Artifacts under The Modern Era). The violet-powered variant proved poisonous to its users and is banned — see Violet Crystals below
- **Blue and violet crystals are not interchangeable.** A device designed for blue crystals will not accept violet, and vice versa — the two energies are not cross-compatible. Each crystal type has its own family of devices, built around its specific properties. Spera's processing facilities and violet-compatible end-use artifacts are a separate category from the blue-crystal-powered devices found in the rest of the world

### Corrupted Magic and Devices

- Magical devices (artifacts, summoning devices, etc.) can function with corrupted magic
- Corrupted magic harms living beings, not mechanical devices
- A device powered by corrupted magic works normally - the harm is to the surroundings, not the device itself

### Blue Crystals (Pure Magic)

- Crystallized pure magic from the seal event
- Safe energy source — processing and use produce no environmental side effects
- **A depleted fossil resource**: blue deposits condensed at concentration points across the continent when the seal was cast (see The Death of Magic). Once ambient magic returned enough for crystals to function and extraction was relearned — centuries ago — nations put blue energy to work quickly, and not only as a luxury: blue crystals supplied magic machines down to the village level in the nations that mastered them. Over the centuries since, the easily accessible deposits were progressively mined out
- **Modern scarcity**: what remains on the surface is the leftovers — scattered deposits one can occasionally still stumble upon, plus harder-to-reach veins. Blue-crystal-powered devices are well-known but have contracted toward major cities and the wealthy as supply thinned while processing costs stayed high
- **The deep reserves**: the richest surviving blue crystal lies underground, in the Hollow cave systems. Magic was densest at depth when the seal froze it, so the deep deposits are the largest and purest on the planet — and the deeper one goes, the purer they get. They remain barely touched: the ecosystem guards them. This is a core part of what makes Hollow hunting profitable (see The Underground Threat → Hollow Hunters)

### Violet Crystals (Corrupted Magic)

- Crystallized corrupted magic from the seal event
- 2 to 5 times more potent than blue crystals as energy (potency varies)
- Concentrated in areas where the infestation was worst during ancient times
- The largest deposit is the prison of the Original organism
- **Unprocessed violet crystals are inert** — they were even used as jewelry in Spera before the breakthrough
- For thousands of years, no one could process violet crystals. Spera's breakthrough 35 years ago finally unlocked their use
- **Every end-use violet device leaks corruption.** Raw violet crystals are inert, but corruption is released when violet crystal energy is converted into usable output by violet-compatible devices. This means lamps, heaters, utility infrastructure, household artifacts, and other violet devices all leak small amounts of corrupted magic while used. Industrial facilities and dense cities are worse because they concentrate thousands of devices in one place, but ordinary citizens using violet energy also contribute.
- **Violet mage batteries — a banned technology.** A mage battery works by feeding energy into the mage's own body (see Using Crystals), and energy drawn from a violet crystal is corrupted magic. Early in the violet era, Spera built violet-powered mage batteries; the mages who used them were absorbing concentrated corruption directly — far beyond anything ambient exposure delivers — and the damage was severe and fast enough that, unlike the slow ambient build-up, cause and effect could not be missed. Within years the batteries were prohibited, and they remain so today; scattered illegal use and a small black market persist. The official rationale confined the lesson to the delivery method — *concentrated violet energy must not be taken into the body; ordinary violet devices are safe* — which preserved violet energy's clean reputation for every other use. It was the violet era's one early, visible harm, and it was explained away. The episode lingers in public memory and feeds quiet conspiracy theories: some say the ban was political; a few whisper that the batteries were only the first symptom of something wrong with violet energy itself. A practical side effect: Speran mages have no legal battery option at home (blue crystal is scarce in Spera), leaving their casting bounded by what they can absorb from ambient air — air that is increasingly fouled
- Spera's industrial scale of violet use is driven by economics, not just supply: violet's 2–5x potency over blue, combined with massive concentrated deposits in Spera's territory, made utility-scale infrastructure economically justified once processing was solved. Blue crystals — even in concentrated deposits — don't carry the same case. Per-crystal output is too low to justify scaling beyond artifact- and machine-level use; conventional energy sources fill the same role at lower cost. So far as anyone knows

## The Modern Era

### Current State

**Technology Level**: High Medieval
- Castles, knights, established kingdoms
- Guilds and structured trades
- Steel weapons and armor

**Magic's Return**: The planet has been producing magic again over millennia. Ambient levels are slowly rising. Magic users have reappeared, though still rare. Current ambient magic levels are roughly 50% of what existed during ancient times — significant enough for mages to practice and crystals to function, but far below the saturation of the old era.

**Ancient Artifacts**:
- Ancient artifacts were designed to draw power from ambient magic and from direct mage injection. They were not built to interface with crystals — there is no socket, no slot, no mechanism for a crystal to feed an ancient artifact directly
- Ancient mages had more mana available than modern mages and could inject more into artifacts on their own
- Over centuries, modern researchers experimented with old artifacts — injecting their own magic and getting partial results (ambient magic today is still below ancient levels, and modern mages have less personal mana than ancient ones)
- This is how people learned the artifacts worked with magic, but a single modern mage rarely has enough magic to power one alone
- **Two practical workarounds exist** for operating ancient artifacts in the modern era:
  - **Multi-mage collaboration**: several mages pool their input into one artifact, combining their reserves
  - **Mage batteries**: the mage uses a crystal-charged battery to top up their reserves on the fly, then channels that boosted energy into the artifact. The crystal does not power the artifact directly — it powers the mage, who powers the artifact
- In either case, the chain still ends with a mage feeding an ancient artifact. Crystals cannot bypass that step

### Discovery of Violet Crystals

**What Was Known:**
- The violet crystal formations have been documented and known for centuries
- They were recognized as different from blue crystals, but nobody could use them

**The Breakthrough:**
- The recent "discovery" was a processing breakthrough - a method to prepare violet crystals so violet-compatible devices could convert their energy into usable output
- This breakthrough occurred 35 years ago
- As with all periods of time, researchers had been trying to solve energy issues and improve quality of life - such experiments eventually yielded success
- This breakthrough unlocked access to a power source 2-5x more potent than blue crystals

**Mining Timeline:**
- Violet crystals are abundant throughout the nation — there was never a need to actively survey for more deposits
- The first years after the breakthrough were spent developing infrastructure: manufacturing mines, equipment, and processing facilities
- Mining started slowly and became more efficient over time
- 35 years of gradual expansion has brought operations close to discovering the largest deposit (the Original's prison)

**Prototype Exports:**
- The processing technique remains a closely guarded secret of Spera
- However, some prototype violet-compatible devices have been sold to wealthy foreign nobles
- These devices saw a warm welcome from those rich enough to afford them
- Due to low local use over a short time, no visible health incidents have occurred outside Spera from these exports

**What the Energy Is Used For:**
- This is NOT industrial in the sense of steam engines or heavy machinery
- Violet crystal energy powers utilities (light, heat), agricultural infrastructure (heated greenhouses, irrigation pumps, growing-season extension in Spera's harsher northern climate), and Spera's family of violet-compatible devices. Violet does not power blue-compatible devices — Spera built its own ecosystem of artifacts and infrastructure around its crystal type. (Violet-powered mage batteries existed early in the violet era — and could channel energy toward ancient artifacts — but they poisoned their users and are now banned; see The Crystals → Violet Crystals)
- Spera became dependent on this energy for basic survival needs - heating homes, lighting cities, and feeding a population grown larger than its arable land could support without violet-assisted agriculture

**Why They Can't Stop:**
- The nation's population grew during the 35 years of violet crystal usage
- The prosperity triggered immigration into Spera from poorer border regions and from people seeking work, safety, or a second chance
- Over 35 years, Spera rebuilt daily life around violet energy. Stopping now would feel less like giving up a luxury and more like cutting electricity, heating, transport, and food infrastructure overnight
- Stopping extraction would cause catastrophic consequences without external help — famine in regions dependent on violet-powered agriculture, deaths from cold in areas relying on violet crystal heating
- The population has grown beyond what conventional energy sources can sustain
- This is an energy crisis, not deliberate villainy
- They unlocked a power source they cannot easily live without, but which is actively poisoning the world

### The Crisis

**What Happened**:
- Mining extracts violet crystals (the extraction itself is not the primary problem)
- Converting violet crystal energy into usable output is what releases corrupted magic
- This conversion happens locally in every end-use violet device - in homes, cities, wherever violet energy is used for heat, light, mage batteries, utilities, or other artifacts
- People fell ill, animals became aggressive, crops failed
- Effects spread from population centers and usage points throughout the nation, not from the mines

**Why It Wasn't Stopped**:
- Early researchers did not know violet crystals were dangerous - small-scale use released too little corrupted magic to cause noticeable harm
- The danger emerged gradually over years of widespread usage as corrupted magic accumulated in the atmosphere
- Health effects began appearing perhaps 15-20 years ago, with gradual increase over time
- By the time patterns emerged and people began to have doubts, the nation was already dependent on violet crystal energy
- This was not immediate knowledge followed by greed - it was slow realization followed by economic and infrastructural inertia
- The corruption is insidious: ordinary citizens using violet energy to heat their homes or light their streets all contribute to the problem

**Current State of Spera**:
- Health issues appearing in the population, especially among active mages
- Growing internal debate about whether violet crystals are the cause
- Researchers being called in to investigate
- Most citizens are not yet alarmed enough to take drastic action
- A rare few have left the country

**Cross-Border Spread**:
- Corrupted magic released into the atmosphere gradually spreads beyond Spera's borders
- Carried by wind and weather patterns over the years
- After 35 years of accumulation, neighboring nations are beginning to experience effects

**The Conflict**:
- Vaeren is in tense conflict with Spera because they believe the spreading corruption is affecting their cities
- War has not yet been officially declared at the story's start - the situation is at the tension/negotiation phase
- War may be officially declared during the game
- Vaeren's religious beliefs (violet crystals as "Tears of the Ancient Devil") add a moral/religious dimension to their objections
- Spera dismisses these concerns as superstition and unproven accusations
- Extraction continues

**Why They Won't Stop**:
- Stopping extraction means losing the conflict, losing the means to sustain the country, losing power
- Those responsible argue that the crystals are NOT causing the problems
- They deny the connection between extraction and the degradation of health, crops, and land
- Official position: the corruption has other causes; the crystals are safe and essential

### Aldemar

- Aldemar is not at war and village life still functions, but the country is under growing pressure from an active Hollow Break
- Refugees, soldiers, and mages pass through parts of the country, including the village region where the player begins
- Not yet directly affected by corruption - the major violet crystal usage centers are in Spera
- Aware of tensions between Spera and Vaeren, but not directly involved
- See the full Aldemar entry under the Nations section

## Calendar System

The world has used two calendars across its history, divided by the single greatest rupture in its timeline: the Death of Magic. Archive fragments should always be dated in whichever calendar was in use at the time of writing.

### The Two Calendars

| Calendar | Span | Reference Point | Length |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ancient Calendar** | Year 1 → Year 1024 | TBD (rooted in the ancient civilization's founding history) | ~1024 years; ends with the Death of Magic |
| **Modern Calendar** | Year 1 → Year 2312 (present) | The Death of Magic itself — the year the seal was cast, after which the world began counting again | ~2312 years so far; currently in use |

**In-world naming and what Year 1 commemorates**: Within the world, Year 1 of the Modern Calendar marks a long-ago catastrophe that everyone counts from — but the *fact* of the reset is universal while the *understanding* of what it commemorates is not. Modern people know the world had a great rupture in deep history and that magic was much stronger before it. The deliberate, planned nature of the Death of Magic — that it was a sacrifice to seal the Original — is the deep mystery the player slowly uncovers. Different cultures use different names for the era: working candidates include "After the Fall," "The Silent Year," "When the Gods Left," or regional/older variants. No single official name dominates, and the player may hear several across their travels.

### Structure

- **12 months**, same names and order as Earth (January through December) — this is a deliberate simplification for player readability
- **~30 days per month**, 365-day year
- In-world, we can assume the ancient civilization developed a calendar that happens to align with ours — no in-game explanation is needed
- **One in-game day ≈ 6,000 steps** — roughly 4,000 steps of day phase and 2,000 of night phase (tunable; the split may eventually vary by season)

### Key Dates

- **Meteor impact**: approximately 2 years before the Death of Magic (Ancient Calendar Year 1022)
- **Death of Magic**: Ancient Calendar Year 1024 — final year of that calendar / Modern Calendar Year 1
- **The catastrophic Vaeren Break and the fall of Selvaren**: Modern Calendar Year 536 — the Hollow Break that broke the Shield, followed by its invasion of Selvaren; the Confluence collapsed in the aftermath, and Aldemar consolidated in the same generation, visibly ending the pact era
- **Current era**: Modern Calendar **Year 2312**
- **Game over (if nothing is done)**: approximately Modern Calendar Year 2315 — when the countdown reaches zero and the Original awakens

### Real-Time Spans

- **Death of Magic to present**: 2,312 years
- **Ancient Calendar duration**: ~1,024 years
- **Modern Calendar duration so far**: ~2,312 years

These final two years before the Death of Magic still unfolded with terrifying speed. Ancient civilization had high magic, teleportation, rapid communication, and emergency logistics, so decisions and mass movement could happen much faster than a modern medieval society could manage. Even so, it was a tight window for everything that had to happen: realization, failed direct attempts on the Original, the decision to seal, hub conversion, mass summoning, pact negotiation, role assignment, population evacuation, and the casting itself.

### Dating Archive Fragments

- **Pre-DoM fragments** → dated in the Ancient Calendar. The ancients kept rigorous records; dates are precise and reliable.
- **Post-DoM fragments** → dated in the Modern Calendar. Records from the early dark age (roughly Modern Years 1–500) are often approximate, contested, or unknown — the calendar's count was preserved as a symbolic anchor by the Keepers and the surviving pact mages, but routine record-keeping collapsed for centuries across most of the continent. Dates from this period frequently rely on guesswork, context, or physical analysis of the materials.
- Fragments written across the Ancient → Modern transition (or in the immediate decades after the seal) may be ambiguous about which system they use — this ambiguity is intentional and usable in-game.

Format example: **"Year 1021 of the [Ancient Calendar Name], August 14th"** — a fragment written shortly before the meteor impact.

### Calendar Names (TBD)

The Ancient Calendar still needs a proper in-world name. For the Modern Calendar, the working assumption is that no single official name dominates — different cultures use different terms (see *In-world naming* above), and the player encounters several variants in the field.

## The Player Character

### Origin

- Isekai - transported from another world (modern Earth or similar)
- No special powers or chosen one status
- An ordinary person — approximately mid-twenties, attended college
- Has parents and a sister, with whom they were close
- Other personal details (name, gender, exact origin, profession) are deliberately left unspecified, and are likely to remain so

### Why They Were Summoned

**The Summoning Devices:**
- Built by the ancients before the Death of Magic
- Derived from teleportation: summoning was developed as an offshoot of the ancients' mature teleportation discipline once the seal decision was made — not invented from nothing inside the crisis window. (This kinship is also why the hub's teleporters could be converted into summoning portals)
- Original purpose: Summon people from non-magical worlds to help survivors rebuild after magic vanished
- The devices detect non-magical worlds specifically - they target realities where magic does not exist
- The devices had no means of selecting for specific skills or knowledge - they only targeted worlds without magic
- The most important thing was that summoned people could live in a world without magic - since many people were summoned, useful knowledge came statistically with the masses
- Automatic function: accumulate magic energy → summon → repeat
- In ancient times, mages fed the devices magic to maximize the summoning rate

**How It Worked in Ancient Times:**
- Devices activated right before/during the Death of Magic
- Summoned people were greeted by guides, explained the situation, given purpose
- These summoned people helped humanity survive the dark age
- They taught survivors how to live without the crutch of magic

**What Happened to the Player:**
- Most summoning devices were deliberately destroyed by otherworlders themselves, 5-10 years after the Death of Magic
- The ancient mages were honest about the situation: magic, in a quantity sufficient to reactivate these devices, would take centuries to return
- Despite knowing this, otherworlders initially held hope that the devices could somehow be used to return home. Many tried for years to make them work — to find any magic
- Hope led to despair. Their people could not move on while clinging to impossible hope
- A small group of otherworlders (a minority, but determined) made the choice to find and destroy the devices, so their people could finally accept this world as home
- The majority probably wanted to keep the devices safe and secured, but this group acted anyway after seeing the depressed state of close relatives clinging to false hope
- These otherworlders were from the northern island group — the ones who had stayed with the mages to cast the seal. They were the only people who knew the physical locations of the devices, since every other group had been teleported away and never traveled the route on foot
- This device survived because it was a prototype — a proof of concept built during the initial research phase, before the hub's conversion to summoning had even begun. The research took place where the institutions and researchers to do it existed — in a city, far from the remote mountain hub — and the prototype was built where that research first succeeded. Only once the principle was proven were the hub's teleporters converted en masse. The northern island group that later destroyed the known summoning devices did not know this prototype existed.
- It is one of the last remaining, possibly the last
- After the Death of Magic, it sat dormant (no magic to accumulate)
- As magic slowly returned to the world, the device accumulated energy - incredibly slowly
- After thousands of years, it finally had enough for one summon
- It activated and pulled the player through
- It's a dumb machine - no awareness that the world changed, no understanding that its purpose is obsolete

**Why the Device Wasn't Discovered:**
- Ancient ruins are everywhere in the world - remnants of the old civilization
- This device was treated as just one more old ruin among many
- The research site was abandoned at the Death of Magic and never resettled. The village the player starts in did not yet exist at the time — the surrounding region was empty for thousands of years before settlers eventually arrived, by which point the device was already overgrown and indistinguishable from countless other ancient ruins
- Over the millennia, individuals likely tried injecting small amounts of magic into it without seeing any result and concluded that it no longer worked
- A summoning requires substantial power, so casual experimentation would yield nothing. That requirement is achievable by modern standards, however: a few appropriately skilled experts actively pouring mana into the prototype could recharge it relatively quickly
- The machine took millennia to charge only because it was abandoned and relying on passive ambient accumulation. Ancient mages actively supplied their devices and were able to summon thousands upon thousands of people
- As an early proof of concept, the surviving prototype may be less efficient than the later mass-converted devices, but raw energy is not the greatest hurdle in understanding or using it

**Why Only One Person:**
- Barely enough accumulated energy for a single summon
- Device is ancient, possibly damaged or degraded
- Was supposed to summon more people
- Was supposed to have guidance waiting

**Why Now - The Correlation:**
- The player's arrival during a time of crisis is not prophecy or destiny
- It is cause and effect: the device spent millennia accumulating energy from the planet's slow return of ambient magic
- The recent increase in ambient magic in the atmosphere, driven in part by Spera's growing use of violet crystals, tipped the device over the activation threshold
- This does not require Aldemar to be heavily corrupted. Trace continent-wide atmospheric change can be enough to finish charging an ancient machine while still being too weak to cause visible health effects in Aldemar, especially with the mountain range reducing direct spread from Spera
- The same violet-crystal economy also pushes mining operations closer to the Original's prison
- The summoning and the potential awakening share the same underlying cause: the processing of violet crystals

**The Player's Situation:**
- Summoned for a purpose that no longer exists (helping a world without magic)
- Arrives in a world where magic has returned
- No guides, no welcome, no explanation - that infrastructure is long gone
- An anachronism, an accident, a glitch in ancient machinery
- Not chosen, not destined - just someone pulled through by a machine blindly following ancient programming
- Any importance they gain is earned through actions, not destiny

### Otherworlder Abilities

**Broad Language Comprehension**: The summoning process grants the ability to understand and speak all languages. This was an imperative — otherworlders were summoned from multiple different worlds and spoke entirely different languages from each other, not just from the natives. Without universal comprehension, otherworlders could not communicate with natives, with each other, or coordinate the survival effort. The summoning imbued this ability automatically.

**A Permanent Rewiring, Not a Sustained Spell**: The gift is woven into the person by the summoning itself — a faculty, not an ongoing enchantment. It works on *any* language, including languages that did not exist when the device was built, and it requires no ambient magic to function. This is why the original otherworlders kept their comprehension through the zero-magic dark age until they died, and why the player understands modern tongues descended from languages the ancients never heard.

**Ancient Language — The Unique Gift**: This broad comprehension includes the Ancient language, which is now extinct. Modern inhabitants cannot read it — the language was lost over millennia as otherworlder languages competed with and eventually replaced it. The player is one of the only people alive who can read Ancient texts, a direct and unique consequence of the summoning process.

**Magic Affinity**: Like all otherworlders, the player has natural sensitivity to magic due to coming from a non-magical world (see Magic System > Otherworlders and Magic).

**Why This Matters Now**: The player is potentially the first person in millennia who can both read Ancient texts AND access magically-sealed archives. They could unlock knowledge that has been inaccessible since the Death of Magic. One prerequisite stands: the player must first learn rudimentary mana manipulation — a low bar, and trivial given their sensitivity, but a required step before any archive opens to them (quest design must respect this gate; see Lost Knowledge and Archives → Why It's Inaccessible).

### Being Discovered

People will eventually deduce the player is an otherworlder from old tales. The signs:
- Strange behavior (doesn't know basic customs or common knowledge)
- Can read Ancient script that no one else understands
- Has magic affinity as an adult with zero training

Any one trait is odd. All three together points to one conclusion.

### The Way Home

**The Goal**: The player wants to return to their world, their home.

**The Portal**: The summoning device/portal that brought the player is located in the forest. After millennia, it is covered by roots, trees, and vegetation - almost not visible anymore. It does not currently function. The villagers know there is *some kind of* old ruin out in the forest — generations have passed it on hunts and gathering trips, and at some distant point in history people likely tried to make it do something and failed — but no one alive in the village understands what it is or pays it any particular attention. It is just one more weathered remnant of the lost civilization, unremarkable in a world full of them.

**The Head Start**: The player begins their search after magic has returned and knows the location of one surviving dimensional device already proven to work: the Caelvenor prototype that brought them here. With access to a few highly specialized experts, the machine can be recharged without an age-long wait. Finding those experts and persuading them to study the device becomes the player's first plausible route home.

**The Directional Problem**: The prototype only knows how to pull someone *into* this world. Recharging it is therefore not enough: if its automatic cycle were allowed to complete unchanged, it would simply perform another inbound summon. The experts must study and safely control or interrupt that cycle while developing a different dimensional mechanism capable of pushing a target out of the world's dimensional space and directing that passage toward the correct destination.

**Outbound Dimensional Technology**: The solution will be a modern invention derived from three bodies of knowledge: the outbound spatial principles recovered from Vaeren's restored Ancient teleportation network, Ancient dimensional theory preserved in Iyenor, and Spera's expertise in modern magic-fueled technology, crystal engineering, and extreme energy concentration. The Ancients wrote the surviving archives; they did not need to rediscover them. They possessed immense magical power in general, but not the post-Death-of-Magic crystals and modern concentration technology needed to package enough energy into this kind of outbound dimensional artifact, nor the time to develop it during the crisis. The artifact's exact crystal type and power interface remain TBD.

**Remaining Unknowns**: The exact construction, calibration, preservation of the player's connection to their home dimension, and limits on how many people can travel remain TBD.

### The Speran Figure (Direction TBD)

At some point in the story, an important figure from Spera will offer to help the player return home. The figure may first be encountered in Vaeren while conducting business or technical work there, with no offer made during that initial meeting. Their identity and motive are not yet established. Two alternative directions remain under consideration:

- **Compromised insider or business figure**: they can connect the player to Speran expertise, but may demand silence, protection, or cooperation concerning corruption, pollution, or other Speran interests
- **Anti-violet radical**: they oppose Spera's use of violet crystals and may demand the player's help with sabotage or terrorist acts intended to stop that use

Neither direction is canon yet. In both, the eventual offer forces the player to weigh their chance of returning home against the methods or interests enabling it.

### Arrival Circumstances

- Appears in a forest, at night, during rain
- Completely disoriented and frightened
- Possibly brief glimpse of their normal life just before transportation (so the player understands what happened)

### Initial Situation

- Attacked by a low-level creature
- Clearly outmatched and terrified
- Rescued by guards who heard the commotion

### How They're Perceived

- Guards see their visible disorientation and fear
- The player's inability to explain themselves ("I have no idea what's going on")
- Their struggle against a low-level threat proves they're not dangerous
- The guards focus on the player's behavior and state, not their strange clothing
- The village is not a war zone, but Aldemar is under pressure from an active Hollow Break. Guards are alert, yet they are also used to displaced, frightened, confused people arriving from unsafe roads and abandoned settlements. This makes the player unusual, but not automatically treated as an enemy.
- Guards conclude: a lost, confused person in need of help — not a threat

### Status

- Brought to the village
- Placed in the care of a local caretaker — someone who helps people in need (lost travelers, the displaced, those who fall through the cracks)
- The caretaker serves as tutorial, helping the player learn how things work
- Player must earn their keep

### The Countdown

- A countdown is displayed in the game UI, starting at approximately 3 years (subject to tuning)
- **Visible to the player, not to the character**: The countdown exists only in the interface. The player character does not see it, sense it, or know it exists. This creates deliberate dramatic irony — the player feels the dread of an impending deadline while the character lives in ignorance until the story reveals what is at stake
- Because one in-game day is roughly 6,000 steps, a three-year countdown represents long-term pressure rather than an immediate time attack
- At the start, the player does not know what the countdown represents or what happens when it reaches zero
- The countdown is tied to the Original's awakening and breakout - when it reaches zero, the Original is awake and breaks out of its prison (growth is the door; see The Alien Organism → Awakening Conditions) and produces new spores. At the story's beginning, existing modern methods cannot replicate the Death of Magic spell or neutralize it, so an unopposed breakout ends the world. The newly invented outbound dimensional artifact is the potential exception the player can help create before the endgame
- The player discovers what the countdown means gradually through the story
- **Baseline tick**: The countdown ticks down passively at a baseline rate driven by Spera's ongoing violet crystal processing and mining operations — these continue regardless of the player. Doing nothing does not freeze the timer; the world's existing trajectory is already pushing toward the awakening
- **Player modulation**: Player choices and actions can accelerate or decelerate that baseline. The countdown is NOT fixed
- **What moves the countdown**: anything that affects either of the two awakening drivers (see The Alien Organism → Awakening Conditions). Player actions only register when they shift demand or supply at scale — a single person using a violet lantern is a rounding error on a continent's atmosphere. The meaningful levers are choices that change violet adoption village-wide, region-wide, or nation-wide, or that affect the mining approach to the prison. Things that *accelerate* it: helping spread violet crystal technology into new markets or settlements, supporting Spera's industrial expansion, contributing to demand growth, or aiding mining operations near the prison. Things that *slow* it: destroying or disrupting violet mines and processing facilities, helping factions opposed to extraction, organizing communities or regions to refuse or abandon violet energy, or any direct interference with the mining approach to the prison
- This is the core tension mechanic: visible, urgent stakes whose meaning unfolds over time

### Visual Transition

- The player arrives in modern clothing from their world
- The caretaker provides the player with local clothing
- This serves as the player's first "equipment" upgrade
- Marks the visual transition into the world - the player now looks like they belong

## The Opening Sequence

1. (Optional) Brief glimpse of player's normal life in their world — walking in the street, sitting at their computer, an ordinary moment cut short
2. Sudden transportation — player appears in the forest at night, in rain, with thunder. The portal that brought them hums slowly into silence behind them
3. Disorientation and fear — player tries to wake themselves up, looks around lost, quickly becomes soaked
4. Strange sounds in the dark; player runs, stumbles, falls. Wet and dirty now
5. A creature emerges and attacks. The player is clearly outmatched and cries for help
6. Guards arrive, drawn by the noise, and dispatch the creature. They are already patrolling because of the Aldemar Hollow Break: the village is not inside the affected zone, but the zone's edge lies only about two to three days' travel away (~12,000–18,000 steps), and villages and cities across the region have adopted prevention measures because some Hollows roam well beyond the zone itself
7. Guards interrogate the player. Seeing their confused and panicked state, and hearing them keep asking what's happening and where they are, the guards take note of the player's name — it sounds foreign to them — and discuss between themselves. A foreign-sounding name alone raises no alarm: villages in Aldemar absorb the occasional drifter, and the northern settlements had their share of settled outsiders before the Break. The guards put the pieces together accordingly: probably one of those — an outsider who had settled in a village to the north, lost everything when the Break hit it, and ran blindly south. This becomes the player's *de facto* cover identity for entering the village — built by the guards themselves, accepted by everyone, requiring no lie from the player
8. Guards escort the player to the village and let them sleep in a temporary building for the night
9. The next morning, the player wakes groggy in the temporary lodging and moves about the small room, disoriented. **Aelis** — the young woman who helps the displaced — appears at the doorframe, having heard them stir, and gently inquires after them, making sure they're all right. She is the first face they see in this world by daylight
10. Tutorial begins under her guidance — earning keep, learning village life, learning how this world works

### Prologue / Act 1 Direction

- The early plot is centered on Aldemar's Hollow Break, not on Spera or the Original. The village still functions, but the wider country has changed posture: patrols, warnings, refugee movement, soldiers and mages passing through, and standing orders to kill wandering Hollows on sight
- The player is pulled into a local survival-and-belonging story first: finding footing, earning keep, learning village life, and understanding why everyone is tense
- The player will hear about Spera's violet-crystal technology during Act 1 before understanding its true danger. It should first appear as distant news, rumor, or something carried by a traveler or visitor from Spera
- Crystal technology should be introduced in contrast: first, the player sees menders/healers using blue-crystal artifacts in an accepted, useful way; later, the player sees someone use a violet-crystal artifact, establishing that Spera's devices feel related but distinct
- The portal/arrival site can be found through optional exploration rather than as a required quest. It is deeply overgrown and easy to miss. There is no meaningful explanation written on the portal itself, but a small ancient stone nearby reads **"Welcome to Caelvenor"** — Caelvenor being the name of the ancient city that stood on this site before the Death of Magic, **not** the modern village's name. The stone is ordinary city signage — a boundary marker of the kind any ancient city would have had — and, by accident of survival, the only “welcome” a summoned arrival receives in place of the guidance that once existed. Aelis (and any other villager) cannot read the Ancient script at all and is unsettled that the player can — the first crack in the player's cover (see Act 1 — Find a Way Back Home)

## Act 1 — Find a Way Back Home

**Main quest spine — "Find a Way Back Home"**: Act 1's through-line is the player's search for a way home, which doubles as the engine that teaches every core mechanic through village and nation life.

**Quest flow:**
1. *Find clues about how you got here* → leads the player to the overgrown portal in the forest
2. Reading the nearby **"Welcome to Caelvenor"** stone — the player understands it is *ancient* precisely because no one living, Aelis included, can read it or recognizes the name. Aelis looks at the player strangely and asks how they could read anything on those structures. This is the player's first sign they are not normal, planted without exposition
3. The quest softens into *"Get your bearings"* — open exploration of the nation — until a late-Act event redirects the spine toward Act 2

**Caelvenor**: the ancient city that stood on the site of the player's starting village before the Death of Magic. The modern village is built on its grave. Whether Caelvenor's specific fate ties into anything larger is open.

**Scope**: Act 1 is not confined to the starting village — the player explores the whole of **Aldemar**: other villages, other people, the nation map. (Other *nations* open only in Act 2.)

**The mounting threat**: Beneath the personal search, Act 1 is about a region bracing for the Hollows. The Aldemar Break is the looming pressure, and preparation is the act's connective tissue. The crown has hired **Vaeren mercenaries** to help contain it (plus participation from neighboring cities), since Vaeren holds the anti-Hollow expertise Aldemar lacks (see Vaeren). These mercenaries — several of them, not one — pass through villages on their way to the front, and the player can meet and talk with some of them in **taverns** (see Taverns). This seeds the wider world and the cross-border dimension before the borders formally open. One specific Vaeren mercenary will become important later (identity TBD; a possible angle: he openly dislikes Aldemar's people, drawing on the old enmity between the nations).

**Climax — the last stand at Holwyn**: scouts spot a huge wave of Hollows massing inside the affected zone — far too large to push back in the open. Riders carry the warning across the two-to-three-day distance well ahead of the wave itself, which moves slowly (feeding, milling, spreading as it comes); that gap is the ~1-week forewarning. The local terrain funnels the wave along a corridor toward Holwyn's area (exact terrain TBD — a valley or forest corridor), and the mercenaries *choose* Holwyn as the fallback point: it offers the best defensibility on the wave's path. They dig in to protect the people — a last stand. The player fights through it shoulder-to-shoulder with the Vaeren mercenary and the village's soldiers — the culmination of the belonging arc.

**Preparation window**: the player gets roughly **a week (~42,000 steps)** of forewarning to prepare before the wave arrives (tunable). How that week is spent feeds directly into the outcome.

**The village stands — but at a cost**: Holwyn survives, but the toll is not fixed. The outcome resolves on a **spectrum driven by the player's Act 1 preparation** — both their personal **strength** (can they hold their part of the line, reach the people who matter?) and the **village's readiness** (how much defense was mounted during the week). The better prepared, the fewer die. In keeping with the world's tone (characters can and do die), specific losses fall out of that:
- **Alira (Aelis's mother) may die** here, depending on the player's actions and preparation — influenceable, not guaranteed either way
- **Most of the mercenary comrades** the player met in the tavern are killed in the defense, though *how many* scales with the preparation too. The one mercenary marked as important later is always among the survivors

**Containment and aftermath**: with the surge repelled, the Break is **contained** and its most dangerous creatures killed — but, per the standing rule, never *fully* eradicated. The routine leak resumes, so manageable monsters keep roaming the nation afterward, handled by ordinary soldiers — which is what keeps everyday combat present across the world (see Modern Hollow Breaks).

**Transition to Act 2**: a convergence opens the borders of the world to the player. By now the player has likely found and read most of the surviving **Iyenor Monoliths** accessible within Aldemar - Ancient inscriptions only they can read - and is hungry for the rest, scattered across the world. Their actions in Holwyn's defense have earned recognition, their surviving **Vaeren mercenary** friend offers a human bridge across the border, and their search for a way home now has a concrete lead: Vaeren's researchers have restored Ancient teleporters and may be the first modern experts capable of understanding the Caelvenor device. The player leaves for Vaeren while also carrying a dawning question: why are the Breaks worsening, and can they be stopped?

## Act 2 — Vaeren and the Teleportation Experts

### Primary Direction

Act 2 takes place mostly in **Vaeren**. The player's immediate personal goal is not yet to solve the planetary crisis, but to find the specialists who can study the Caelvenor prototype and determine whether a route home is possible. Vaeren's success in restoring part of the Ancient teleportation network makes it the strongest available lead.

### Earning Access

The relevant knowledge is not handed to the player on arrival. They must explore Vaeren, earn the respect and trust of its inhabitants, and meet - potentially befriend - the researchers whose cooperation they need. The surviving Vaeren mercenary from Holwyn provides an introduction, not an automatic solution. The specific institutions, researchers, locations, and sequence of trust-building remain TBD.

### Why Vaeren Has the Experts

Vaeren inherited the Shields' long engagement with the Hollows. The working explanation is that research around underground exits, threshold wards, spatial boundaries, and anchoring led Vaereni specialists toward dimensional magic and helped them reactivate some Ancient city terminals. The exact historical and technical bridge between these disciplines remains to be developed. Their restored-teleporter expertise nevertheless makes them the strongest first interpreters of the player's summoning device and establishes the technological foundation for later outbound-dimensional research.

### Restoring the Network

Working with these researchers can unlock the player's ability to identify and reactivate compatible Ancient terminals elsewhere in the world. Each restored city terminal connects through the mountain hub and adds another selectable destination. This can form an Act 2 side-quest line and a gradually expanding fast-travel system; its exact objectives and costs remain TBD.

### The Wider Pressure

While the player pursues this personal goal, the threat around Vaeren continues to grow. Hollow activity worsens day by day, and rumors or evidence of Spera's strange pollution become increasingly common. These pressures draw the way-home story into the larger crisis without requiring the player to begin the act already committed to saving the world.

### Toward Act 3

Vaeren can recover the outbound principle, but cannot complete an interdimensional solution alone. The research points onward toward **Iyenor's** preserved Ancient theory and **Spera's** modern expertise in magic-fueled technology, crystal engineering, and energy concentration. Which crystal system can safely be used, and how it interfaces with the artifact, remain TBD. Act 2 may also introduce the important Speran figure while they are conducting business or technical work in Vaeren, before any offer is made. Their identity, role, motive, and precise timing remain TBD (see Notes & Plot Hooks).

## Established Endgame Direction

### The Dimensional Artifacts

Returning home and containing the Original ultimately depend on newly produced **outbound dimensional artifacts**. These are not recovered Ancient devices: they are modern achievements combining Vaeren's restored teleportation principles, Iyenor's Ancient records, and Spera's crystal-engineering and energy-concentration expertise. They provide the outward force needed to push a target out of this world's dimensional space. Current ending structure assumes that each artifact enables one such outward displacement. Whether an artifact operates independently, through a modified Caelvenor prototype, or as part of another apparatus remains TBD, as do its exact mechanism, crystal system, and limits.

### Containing the Original

The Original remains indestructible. The intended containment is to exile it into a closed, isolated dimensional space rather than kill it or use the player's portal as a seal. Constructing and targeting that exile still requires strong dimensional magic, and its exact mechanics remain TBD.

### Production as the Branch Point

If only one artifact is completed before the Speran production site, its experts, or crucial knowledge is lost, the player cannot accomplish both outward displacements. The cause of that loss remains TBD - possibilities include terrorism, monsters, war, or another consequence of the player's earlier choices - and rebuilding the capability would take decades in the damaged region.

If at least two artifacts are completed or preserved, it becomes possible to exile the Original and still retain another means of sending the player home. The number produced is therefore the central endgame resource, while the choices that protect or destroy production remain to be designed.

### Ending Structure

- **Two or more artifacts**: the Original is exiled, and the player can choose freely whether to remain or return home. Bringing companions may also be possible, subject to capacity rules and magic-withdrawal consequences still TBD
- **One artifact, containment chosen**: the artifact is used to exile the Original, so the player must remain in this world unless or until the lost capability can someday be rebuilt
- **One artifact, return chosen**: the player uses the sole artifact to flee home, potentially trying to bring others, but the Original cannot be contained and the world is lost
- **No usable artifact**: the player cannot return home and the Original cannot be contained - the game-over branch

In branches with at least one usable artifact, the player's final irreversible choice at the game's climax is whether to go home or remain. Earlier play determines whether that choice must be made against the survival of the world or can be made freely.

### Continental Geography

- A major east-west mountain range divides the continent into northern and southern halves
- The ancient teleportation hub ruins sit at the center of this mountain range
- A river flows northward through the mountains from the hub area to the northern coast
- Additional rivers flow south from the mountains
- Several islands off the coasts, including a major one to the north — the modern Iyenor (Keepers Nation), settled by the seal-casters and accompanying otherworlders after the Death of Magic. See the Iyenor entry below
- Small nations or zones exist at the western edge, eastern coast, and southern area - their exact nature (independent nations, absorbed remnants, or autonomous zones) is TBD

### Naming Conventions (Modern Nations)

Modern names descend from otherworlder tongues drifted over ~2,300 years (see Names and Naming under Ancient History for the shared aesthetic). Beyond that common root, each nation has its own naming culture:

- **Aldemar** — worn, homely, half-familiar names; naming is a *personal and familial* matter, not an institutional one (Aelis, Alira, Soren; the village Holwyn). Small family customs are common — Aelis's parents framing her name from their own initials is an example of how individual such choices can get
- **Vaeren** — the most *ordered and traditional* names, reflecting the nation's deep military heritage: formal and continuous, more so than its neighbors (fitting a nation that even kept its own ancient name across millennia). Specific conventions still to be defined
- **Spera** — *no convention is the convention.* Reinvention is the culture: people starting a new life there conventionally take a **new name** on arrival (not enforced, but near-universal), and Speran names follow no rules — plain word-names like "Blue," "Leaf," or "Sky" are perfectly ordinary, and children are given the same free range. Family names are rare, usually shed with the past. (Whether and how Speran identity is recognized — and whether faking it is even a concern — is an open question; see Open Questions)

### Aldemar

- A pact-fragment fusion nation. Not a direct successor to any single original pact nation — Aldemar is what consolidation produced
- **Composition**: Sower-core (descended primarily from Maerith), with absorbed Mariner fragments (Aolanir) along the coast and minor Shaper inheritance (Kaldun) from the mountain frontier with Spera. None of these original nations exists today as such
- **Founding pressure**: Forged defensively in the dark age against the rising military aggression of Vaeren (the Shield successor). Vaeren faced an existential Hollow surge when the wards broke at the Death of Magic and over-militarized to survive. That militarization eventually scaled past defense and pressured neighbors, leaving the smaller pact-fragments adjacent to it no choice but to consolidate. Aldemar is the result
- **The geography of that war**: Vaeren is northeast and Aldemar is southwest, on opposite ends of the continent. Vaeren's expansion reached the southwestern pact-fragments by pushing through what would only centuries later become Spera — at the time, an unsettled, abandoned no-man's-land known to outsiders as **the Cinderlands** (see Spera under Nations), which no surviving pact nation claimed. The feared portion of that region was the specific area around the Original's lair, not the whole territory, so Vaeren's armies could move through the empty northern and southern stretches without contesting it. Mountain passes and coastal sea routes were also used. The geographic distance did not protect the southwest; it only delayed the pressure long enough for the surviving fragments to recognize the threat and consolidate
- **The trigger — Selvaren's fall**: The proximate cause of the alliance was Vaeren's invasion and destruction of **Selvaren**, the original Mender nation. The invasion did not come from nowhere — Vaeren had been visibly straining for generations under its uniquely brutal conditions, and Selvaren had tried repeatedly to raise those concerns at the Confluence without the other nations taking them seriously (see Vaeren → Why the Shield Turned). When the Shield finally broke and seized Selvaren's safer lands, watching one pact nation erase another pact nation was the wake-up call: if the Shield could end the Menders, no smaller fragment would survive alone. Selvaren refugees scattered; some carried medical knowledge into the Maerith-Aolanir-Kaldun fragments that became Aldemar, but the institutional depth of the Mender tradition did not survive — which is part of why modern medicine across the world is shallower than its ancient inheritance would suggest
- The name **Aldemar** is provisional — may be revised as the nation's identity is refined further
- Located in the southwest, south of the mountain range
- **Government**: a kingdom ruled by a **Queen**. The King died of illness some years ago, and there is currently no king
- Where the player arrives and starts
- Aldemar is not at war and ordinary village life still functions, but the country is under growing pressure from an active Hollow Break
- Refugees, soldiers, and mages visibly move through affected regions and nearby villages
- Not yet directly affected by corruption — the major violet crystal usage centers are in Spera
- Aware of the tensions between Spera and Vaeren, but not directly involved
- Old, cold enmity with Vaeren persists as cultural memory from the founding war, but is no longer hot — the two nations have not been actively at war for a long time
- Contains: the player's starting village, built on the ruins of the ancient city **Caelvenor** (see Act 1 — Find a Way Back Home). The rest of the nation map (other villages, regions) is to be developed; the location assets currently in the project are placeholders

**Geography:**
- A mountain range separates Aldemar from Spera, acting as a natural border
- This geographic barrier helps explain why corruption has not spread significantly into Aldemar

### Holwyn (the Starting Village)

The village where the player arrives and where Act 1 takes place. It sits in Aldemar, on the overgrown ruins of the ancient city **Caelvenor** — which the villagers neither remember nor find remarkable, since ancient ruins are common everywhere (see Act 1 — Find a Way Back Home).

- **Name**: "Holwyn" is a worn-down modern name; its buried sense is *the holly-wood / the quiet green*. Like real place names, the original meaning has eroded over the centuries — it sounds peaceful without announcing why
- **Age**: founded roughly **2–3 centuries ago**. The surrounding region lay empty for thousands of years after the Death of Magic; Holwyn is a comparatively young, plainly modern settlement, dwarfed in age by the ~2,300-year-old ruins beneath it. Old enough to feel timeless and deeply rooted to those who live there, far too young to touch the ancient world
- **Why it exists**: a quiet pocket of good land — fertile soil, forest for timber, water for fishing, a little ore in the nearby hills — set well off the main, state-guarded trade routes. Settled by people who wanted a simple life and to be left alone
- **Off the trade routes, on the way to the Break**: Holwyn is not on any trade route, but it is accessible — it lies along the natural approach from one of the great roads toward the Break zone. That is why soldiers, mages, and mercenaries now pass through a village that rarely saw through-traffic before, and why the climax's Hollow wave, funnelled by the same terrain, comes Holwyn's way (see Act 1 — Find a Way Back Home)
- **Its character**: an ordinary, peaceful village — quiet, self-sufficient, slow-paced. People here mind their own work and want for little. It is *not* a refuge by vocation; it simply happens, now and then, to absorb a drifter or a displaced person who wandered through and chose to stay, because it's a calm place where someone with nowhere else to go can settle unbothered. (That incidental quality is what lets the player slip in, and what gives Aelis room to do what she does — but it isn't the town's mission)
- **The double edge**: the quiet isolation that has kept Holwyn untroubled for generations is the same isolation that leaves it exposed now, as the Aldemar Hollow Break intrudes on a place that has known calm for as long as anyone alive remembers

### Spera (The Violet Nation)

- **Not a pact nation** - a completely separate origin
- **Name origin**: "Spera" means hope. Named only a few hundred years ago — the first nation of its kind: not a successor, fragment, or consolidation of any earlier polity, but a people self-assembled from castoffs; the name carries no pact-era lineage. The name was chosen by the first great groups who attempted to unite the scattered people hiding within this land - refugees from wars, exiles, outcasts with nowhere else to go. Before this effort, the territory was a no-man's land with no shared identity or unity. The act of naming gave these disparate people a sense of belonging and a collective identity for the first time.
- **Pre-Spera name — "The Cinderlands"**: Before unification, the region had no name of its own (its scattered inhabitants had only local settlement names, if any). The dismissive collective name used by *outsiders* — the surrounding nations who would never have set foot in it — was **the Cinderlands**. The name had a literal origin: in the years before the Death of Magic, the Original's feeding saturated a wide zone around its lair with such dense corruption that all life died (see Corrupted Magic → The Dead-Zone Exception). When the seal was cast, that whole zone froze in place: barren ground, dried-out tree skeletons, no animals, no growth — studded with the violet crystal formations into which the surrounding corruption had crystallized. To anyone who saw it (or heard the accounts that spread across the post-DoM continent), it looked exactly like burnt-out land — a country of cinder. Nature has slowly returned over the 2,300 years since, and much of the wider region is ordinary land again, but the name stuck and is still occasionally heard today, often pejoratively, when older or more traditional speakers refer to Spera or its people. The original reason the area was avoided (proximity to the Original's lair) had been forgotten by the time the name solidified into a generic insult, but the inherited sense of "cursed, empty, not for us" persists.
- **Ruins in Spera**: ancient ruins exist here as they do everywhere on the continent — but those in the long-avoided stretches are often better preserved than the continent's average, left genuinely untouched for millennia instead of quarried and repurposed. Spera's growth over the last century — explosive in the 35 violet years — has begun to change that: ruins near expanding cities and mining districts are increasingly stripped, dismantled, or built over
- Located in the north-center of the continent, north of the mountain range
- **Government**: no monarch. Governed by a ruling trio — two men and one woman — under a form of democracy that is steadily growing more corrupt
- Formed by outcasts, exiles, the too-poor, the banned - people with nowhere else to go
- Settled the land nobody wanted: the most corrupted, most feared territory after the Death of Magic, full of violet crystal deposits and the lingering trauma of the organism
- The territory was genuinely abandoned and empty — no pact nation claimed it, and it was not the remnant of a fallen pact nation. It was reclaimed over thousands of years by people fleeing their own countries for any reason
- Contains the largest violet crystal deposit (the Original's prison) - though it remains undiscovered
- Industrial-scale violet crystal use caused the corruption
- Experiencing growing internal tensions and health issues, though most citizens are not yet alarmed
- A rare few have left the country over the years for various reasons
- Crime was historically very high - a frontier society built from desperate people
- Known cultural fact: accepts anyone within its borders. The *why* has been largely forgotten over the centuries; it was simply born of necessity
- **Naming custom**: newcomers conventionally take a *new name* on arrival, and Speran names follow no rules at all — reinvention is woven into the culture (see Naming Conventions)
- The breakthrough in violet crystal processing (35 years ago) was their first real prosperity as a nation - a people who had always been the unwanted suddenly thriving
- Visual identity: steampunk-ish - violet crystal energy powering an industrial infrastructure unlike anything else in the world
- From their perspective, the political crisis feels like the old established nations attacking them again, as they always have

**Geography:**
- The capital and major cities are located in the far north-east of the nation
- This is where violet crystal use is densest, making the north-east the primary source of corruption
- Aldemar lies to the south, separated by mountains
- Vaeren lies to the east; their western population centers sit close to Spera's eastern cities, placing them nearest to the corruption's source

**Why Blue Crystals Aren't an Alternative:**
- The nation has very few blue crystal deposits
- This is because the Original's presence meant most magic in that region was already corrupted before the Death of Magic
- When magic crystallized, the ratio was skewed: mostly violet crystals, very little blue
- Before discovering how to process violet crystals, they were a poor and marginalized nation — while other nations had ridden blue energy through centuries of development, Spera never had that option
- They survived on their few blue crystal quarries and conventional energy sources (coal, etc.)
- The breakthrough in violet crystal processing transformed them - the nation grew, and its people enjoyed prosperity for the first time
- Now they cannot easily go back

### Vaeren

- A pact-nation descendant — direct successor to the ancient Vaeren (the Shield nation), one of the original six pact nations. The modern nation has retained the ancient name across millennia, an unusual cultural continuity
- Pact role (ancestral): Underground creature containment, fortification, combat techniques
- Located in the northeast of the continent
- **Government**: a kingdom ruled by a **King**
- Sits atop the densest concentration of Hollow exits on the continent — the geographic basis of the original Shield role
- In tense conflict with Spera due to spreading corruption affecting their cities
- War has not yet been officially declared at the story's start

**Why the Shield Turned — The Invasion of Selvaren**:

When the Death of Magic broke the wards on dangerous underground exits, Vaeren faced an existential creature surge. Survival demanded heavy militarization, and the pact's role-distribution had matched Vaeren with the most warrior-minded otherworlders — populations chosen to fit the harshest assignment. It worked, for a while.

But the conditions never softened. Generation after generation of Vaereni inherited a life that was simply harder than what their cousins in the other pact nations lived. Children grew up watching parents die young to Hollows, and asking why their nation alone bore that cost while the others farmed, healed, sailed, and preserved in relative safety. Resentment grew slowly, across generations, the way grievances do when they are real and structural rather than acute.

Vaeren raised the issue at the Confluence repeatedly. **Selvaren** in particular heard them and tried to relay the concerns. By that era, however, the Confluence was already being looked down upon by the younger generations of the other nations — its sharing aspect was no longer universally appreciated, and the spirit of mutual obligation had thinned. Vaeren's complaints were not heard, or not taken seriously.

Then, in Modern Year 536, a particularly catastrophic Hollow Break struck — worse than any in living memory. Two things made it so devastating. First, the Shield was holding the line without magic — exactly the future the role had been created for. The pact's planners foresaw that the wards would die with the seal and the Hollows would become a real threat once again; the Shield existed precisely for that, and Vaeren was set in front of it deliberately. That is the root of the role's tragedy: what the ancients had handled with mastered magic — warded exits, a managed underground — Vaeren held with steel, walls, and bodies. Against the routine leak, it sufficed; against what came in 536, it could not. Second — though no one alive could have known it — the deep had begun to stir: magic returns from the planet's core outward, and by that era the first traces of recovery were reaching the deepest caves, centuries before anything would be perceptible at the surface. Creatures that had slumbered since the seal were starting to move again, and the pressure they put on everything above them drove unusual numbers upward. The Hollows of that Break were far from the strongest the underground holds — against a magicless Shield, they did not need to be. Something broke in Vaeren. Their next move was not another defensive push but the **invasion of Selvaren**. Selvaren occupied the fertile central-eastern lowlands directly southwest of Vaeren — temperate, well-watered, easy to cross — which made it both the natural first target for relieving the pressure and a strategic gateway deeper into the continent. The reasons were both straightforward and bitter: Selvaren held safer, gentler territory that would relieve the pressure, and Selvaren had been the one nation that *almost* listened — which made the deafness of the others sharper by contrast. Vaeren took the land. Selvaren as a polity ended.

This is what triggered the formation of Aldemar (see Aldemar above) and finished off the Confluence. The institution had already been weakening for generations — younger generations in the other nations had grown indifferent or hostile to the sharing principle, attendance had thinned, and commitment had eroded — but the invasion was the final shock that ended it outright. The pact era closed there.

**Modern Era**: The militarism remains as cultural inheritance but is no longer expansionist. The lived grievance of those generations is not the daily experience of modern Vaereni — they are a settled medieval nation now, with a hard reputation and a long memory. The current conflict with Spera draws on the old "fight evil from below the earth" identity rather than any new expansionist hunger.

**Geography:**
- Population centers are located in the west of the nation (their western border)
- This places them closer to the corruption spreading from Spera's eastern cities

**Religious Beliefs — "Tears of the Ancient Devil"**:
- Vaeren has a religious tradition tied to their oral heritage
- Violet crystals are called "Tears of the Ancient Devil" — tears shed by an evil being as it was defeated
- According to their belief, the Ancient Devil was defeated by mages of old
- Anything related to the devil is considered taboo
- This belief is mainstream in their religion, not a fringe view
- They have held this belief for thousands of years
- The religion fits the ancient Shield role: Vaeren has always defined itself by fighting evil from below the earth — first the Hollows, now the corruption rising from violet crystal processing

**Truth Preserved Through Distortion**:
- The "Ancient Devil" corresponds to the Original organism
- The "Tears" are crystallized corrupted magic
- "Defeated by mages" refers to the ancients sealing magic away
- The myth is wrong in details but correct in essence: violet crystals ARE dangerous, they ARE connected to something evil, and messing with them IS a bad idea

**Historical Dismissal**:
- Spera never accepted these beliefs
- They lived on their land for thousands of years without consequence from the unprocessed crystals
- Violet crystals were even used as jewelry in Spera — unprocessed crystals are harmless
- The religious warnings were dismissed as superstition
- Now that effects are appearing, Vaeren feels vindicated

### Iyenor (The Keepers Nation)

- A pact-nation descendant — the smallest and most fragile surviving pact nation
- Located on the northern island
- Barely surviving as a culture in the modern era

**Founding**: See Ancient History → Pact Nation Roles → The Keepers' Special Case for the full account of how the population, the role, and the destination were planned and assembled. Two details specific to Iyenor that are not covered there:
- Whatever native islanders existed before the migration were a small minority, absorbed into the dominant migrant group
- The name **Iyenor**, in the Ancient language, was retained or adopted retrospectively as the role solidified across generations

**What They Have**:
- The original ancient archives, teleported to the island before the seal — written in Ancient on the durable materials the old civilization used, unreadable by anyone alive today
- Post-Death-of-Magic records the Keepers themselves began producing in the dark age and continued across the centuries: chronicles of what happened during the collapse, the founding of the pact nations, the Confluence years, and the slow drift of the modern era. Their role was always to preserve both ancient history and ongoing history, not just one
- Fragments of truth about the ancient era — not complete knowledge, but pieces that survived in distorted form
- A cultural tradition of preservation, even if the reason behind it has faded over millennia
- The legacy of the **Iyenor Monoliths** project (see Ancient History → The Iyenor Monoliths): during the Confluence centuries, Iyenor sent emissaries across the world to erect Ancient-language stone markers as a redundant, durable record. Most are now lost or destroyed, but the surviving monoliths are scattered across the continent rather than concentrated on the island

**What They Lost**:
- The full understanding of what happened and why
- The Ancient language itself, eventually. It survived among the Keepers longer than anywhere else, but its death there was overdetermined:
  - **Founding demographics**: the island's founders were majority otherworlder — a few hundred of them to ~100 mages, and those mages spent the early years weakened by withdrawal, dependent on the very people they had summoned. The otherworlders could all *speak* Ancient through the gift, but the gift dies with its bearer: their children learned whatever was actually spoken at home and in the street
  - **Comfort languages**: the otherworlders deliberately kept their own tongues — the languages of lost homes, held onto as comfort and identity. The weakened, guilt-ridden mages were in no position to impose linguistic discipline, and had no wish to
  - **Nothing to read it for**: the sealed archives could not be opened for the better part of a millennium — there was no magic to open them with. Generation after generation, fluency had no object; the duty contracted from *reading the collection* to *guarding the objects*
  - **Internal strife**: Iyenor was not spared the dark age — internal conflicts flared across the centuries. As early as the first decade, the community split over the summoning devices: a determined otherworlder faction left the island and traveled back to the hub to destroy them, against the likely wishes of the majority — a decision the island surely fought over when they returned. Much later, at least one major attack on the island (see below) destroyed records and killed people who carried more of the language than anyone realized
  - By the time ambient magic returned enough to open a seal, the fluent were long dead. What remained was ritual: reverence for texts no one could read
- Significant portions of their post-DoM written records, lost to damp, fire, neglect, and at least one major attack on the island that destroyed an important section of the collection
- Wars, plagues, and the slow grind of the dark age destroyed other written archives elsewhere on the continent and killed people who possessed more detailed knowledge
- Over millennia, the "why" behind their preservation duty degraded while the duty itself persisted as cultural habit

**Narrative Role**: This is where the player could eventually learn the truth of the world — or most of it — after having gathered glimpses and fragments elsewhere. The player's ability to read the Ancient language makes these archives accessible for the first time in millennia. The Keepers have been guarding answers they could not read.

## The Weekly Newspaper

A weekly in-game newspaper used to deliver world events to the player. **Each nation publishes its own paper**, so content varies by the nation the player is in.

**Production**: Newspapers exist because simple copying artifacts make limited print runs possible. They are not modern industrial newspapers; they are weekly state, guild, or city sheets copied through magical tools and distributed along established routes.

**Delivery**: Once per in-game week. The player picks it up or subscribes to it from a dedicated location within town (specific mechanism TBD).

**Content**: Recent events, warnings about dangerous monsters in the area, and other news the player should know. Some content shifts based on the player's choices.

**Archive**: All received newspapers are stored and re-readable. Slots into "The Book" (the planned Journal/Compendium UI — see CLAUDE.md, "Planned Features").

## Archive Fragments

In-world textual fragments from archives, records, journals, inscriptions, and other written sources the player may encounter. These are canonical pieces of the world's written history and should be treated as diegetic content, not external references (those belong in `Documentation/Quotes.md`).

**Two categories** based on when the fragment was written:

- **Before the Death of Magic** — Written during the ancient civilization. Dates are precise and reliable because the ancients kept rigorous records and used a stable calendar system. Language is Ancient (unreadable to most modern people; the player can read it due to the summoning).
- **After the Death of Magic** — Written during or after the dark age. Dates are often approximate, contested, or unknown. The calendar system broke down; record-keeping collapsed; languages fragmented. Attribution and dating may rely on guesswork, context, or physical analysis of the materials.

### Entry Format

Each fragment should include:

- **Title / Identifier**: Short descriptive name
- **Date**: Use the calendar in effect at the time of writing (see Calendar System section). Ancient Calendar for pre-DoM fragments, Modern Calendar for post-DoM fragments (including dark-age fragments — dates from that period are often approximate or contested). Format: "Year X of the [Calendar Name], Month Day".
- **Origin**: Where found, who wrote it if known
- **Language**: Ancient, early post-DoM dialect, modern, etc.
- **Text**: The actual content of the fragment
- **Context / Notes**: What it reveals, hints at, or connects to. Any metadata relevant for placement in game.
